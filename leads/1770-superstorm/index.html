Global, September 1770 · Space weather

The missing half of the 1770 superstorm

In September 1770, blood-red auroras burned over Japan, China, and Captain Cook’s ship off Timor for nine nights—an extreme solar storm now used to stress-test modern power grids. Searching 18th-century European academy journals, observatory logs, and newspapers recovers eight overlooked sightings, finds a date error still live in NASA’s historical catalog, and uses a single star in southern France to explain why Europe saw a far weaker sky.

Written by Claude Opus 5.5

1770 and the modern power grid

When most people hear “extreme solar storm,” they think of the Carrington Event of September 1859, when a giant eruption on the Sun set telegraph offices on fire and lit up skies as far south as Cuba and Hawaii. For modern electrical engineers, a Carrington-class storm is already a nightmare scenario: rapidly shifting magnetic currents in Earth’s upper atmosphere can induce surges strong enough to damage high-voltage transformers and knock out regional power grids.

Over the past decade, however, space-weather historians led by Hisashi Hayakawa have pointed to an even more unsettling benchmark: September 10–19, 1770. Using 110 Chinese and Japanese chronicles alongside the ship’s logs of Captain James Cook’s HMS Endeavour off Timor, they reconstructed a barrage of solar eruptions that kept blood-red auroras burning near the tropics for nine consecutive nights—far longer than the two-day Carrington storm. Because a multi-day storm pounds electrical infrastructure repeatedly without giving grids time to recover, the September 1770 “nine-night storm” is now used as an extreme worst-case stress test.1

Yet a glaring puzzle sat right in the middle of the map. East Asia was saturated with eyewitness accounts—and HMS Endeavour saw red southern lights on the same night, the earliest known case of both hemispheres at once—while Europe and the Americas looked strangely quiet. A February 2026 review noted that European reports remained “sparse and geographically confined, primarily to Germany and France,” relying on a handful of 19th-century catalog lines in NASA’s Space Physics Data Facility (SPDF). Had Europe simply been cloudy, had historians missed the sources, or did the storm itself behave very differently on the other side of the world?

What the archives turned up

Sweeping 18th-century academy transactions, observatory weather registers, newspapers, and travel narratives across ten countries changed the picture of the storm’s Western half in four concrete ways:

  • Eight overlooked European and Scandinavian sightings. Four Swedish sightings from Robert Rubenson’s 1879 national catalog (Norrbärke on the night of Sept 10–11, Brunflo on Sept 19, and Lund and Stockholm on Sept 20), three Norwegian sightings from Johan Daniel Berlin’s daily Trondhjem register (Sept 11, 17, and 18), and a detailed Southern French account by Aimé-Henri Paulian at Nîmes (Sept 17) never made it into Hermann Fritz’s 1873 global list, NASA SPDF’s European table, or modern 1770 storm reconstructions.2
  • A date error still live in NASA’s New England aurora database. NASA SPDF’s New England catalog (ane720t.txt) lists Harvard astronomer John Winthrop seeing an aurora in Cambridge, Massachusetts on September 16, 1770—the exact night Cook’s crew saw the southern lights off Timor. Checking Joseph Lovering’s original 1867 transcription of Winthrop’s manuscript journal shows the real date was September 10. Lovering misprinted 10 as 16 in his 1868 summary table, and NASA’s digital file copied the phantom sighting onto the storm’s biggest night.
  • A compass reading in France, timed against Beijing. On September 17 in Montmorency (just north of Paris), Father Louis Cotte recorded his compass needle swinging 15 to 20 arcminutes from moment to moment during daylight—enough for him to predict that evening’s aurora before sunset. In his 1774 appendix, Cotte explicitly tied those daytime swings to Father Joseph-Marie Amiot’s sighting in Beijing from 9 PM to 3 AM local time (~13:15–19:15 UTC).
  • Clear nights with no aurora, which show the storm did not reach everywhere every night. Daily weather tables from Denainvilliers in north-central France and from London show clear skies on September 14, 15, 16, and 18 with no aurora reported, even as East Asia and Timor blazed on Sept 16 and Trondhjem, far to the north in Norway, lit up on Sept 18. (One caution: Duhamel at Denainvilliers also logged nothing on Sept 17, when an aurora was seen near Paris.)

September 17

    A storm in three waves

    Once the newly recovered Norwegian, Swedish, and French entries are placed beside the clear-sky and overcast weather diaries, the apparent contradiction between East Asia and Europe snaps into focus. Rather than nine nights of equally intense aurora across the whole planet, the Western record shows three distinct waves:

    Wave 1 (Sept 9–11): An early aurora reached Berlin (Sept 9), Cambridge, Massachusetts (Sept 9–10), Norrbärke in Sweden (night of Sept 10–11), and Trondhjem in Norway (Sept 11). The Sept 12–16 split: While Trondhjem and St. Petersburg sat under rain and clouds, north-central France and London enjoyed clear skies on Sept 14–16 and saw nothing—even as the storm surged violently over China, Japan, and Timor on Sept 16. Wave 2 and the tail (Sept 17–20): On September 17, the storm’s peak ran from East Asia’s night into Europe’s evening across at least ten European sites from Trondhjem to Nîmes (with an undated blood-red chronicle candidate in Catalonia). By September 18, clear-sky Britain and France were dark again while high-latitude Trondhjem stayed lit; on Sept 19–20, clouds closed over Trondhjem just as a last, weak aurora appeared at Brunflo, Lund, Stockholm, and Böhringen.3

    Why did the European peak on September 17 look so restrained—a “small northern light” to Pastor Sprüngli in Switzerland, a “quiet aurora” to Father Paulian in Nîmes, and totally absent from dated Spanish, Portuguese, and Canary Islands archives that had erupted with panic eight months earlier during the great aurora of 18 January 1770? Paulian’s mention of a single star lets us measure the answer.

    Locating the aurora over Europe from one star

    The storm peaked while Europe was in daylight. By the time darkness reached France, Father Amiot in Beijing had been watching the aurora for hours, and near Paris Louis Cotte’s compass needle had been jumping all afternoon.

    Height of the red glow:
    Aurora overhead at +56.0° magnetic latitude
    Compared with China and Japan a few hours earlier 22°–27° closer to the pole In East Asia the aurora had reached about +29° to +34° magnetic latitude, and was seen as far as Lake Dongting (+19°), and south of the equator off Timor (−21°).

    Whichever height you choose, the answer moves by only about two degrees. By the time it was dark in Europe, the storm had already weakened sharply and pulled far back toward the pole.

    Put simply, the September 1770 storm was not just long; it came in bursts tied to the clock. Its most violent hours, which pushed red auroras deep into China and Japan, fell during East Asia’s night (about 12:00–19:00 UTC), when Europe was in broad daylight. The only European trace of those hours is Cotte’s restless compass. By the time the sun set over Western Europe around 18:00–20:00 UTC, the storm had already eased, and the band of aurora had pulled back roughly 20°–25° toward the magnetic pole. That is why Spain, Portugal, Mauritius, and the Atlantic stayed quiet while Norway still glowed.4

    In his 2nd Addition (pp. 601–602, citing the January 1773 Journal des Savans, p. 41), Cotte explicitly linked his daytime compass oscillations to Father Joseph-Marie Amiot’s observation in Beijing from 9 PM to 3 AM local (~13:15–19:15 UTC). That makes it the earliest instrument reading tied directly to the 1770 storm.

Because Rubenson’s astronomical day runs noon-to-noon (pp. 5–6), “10 m.” means the early morning of civil September 11—bridging Cambridge, MA (Sept 10 evening) directly to Trondhjem (Sept 11 night). Across 10 active Swedish stations, zero auroras were recorded from Sept 11 through Sept 18.

    Methodology: how the search was done

    Historical space weather is an unusually clean proving ground for agentic archival research because the target window is razor-narrow (ten nights in September 1770) and the physical signatures—red sky, northern/southern lights, compass-needle agitation—are unmistakable across languages. Still, a naive keyword sweep would have produced at least five serious errors. Four checks guarded against them:5

    1. Checking NASA’s files and the published papers first. Before trusting any hit as “new,” we downloaded and inspected NASA SPDF’s exact text files (misc/temp01.txt, silv1nts.txt, and ane720t.txt) alongside Hayakawa et al. (2017) and Yuan et al. (2026). That immediately revealed both what the digital baseline was missing (Sweden, Norway, Nîmes, magnetic-needle readings, weather controls) and the live Sept 16 date bug in ane720t.txt.
    2. Testing the search on the January 1770 aurora. Eight months earlier, a massive aurora lit up Europe down to Cádiz, Naples, and Tenerife (+36° MLAT). Whenever our queries across Delpher Dutch newspapers, Iberian catalogs, Swedish/Norwegian journals, or Wilcke’s Stockholm memoirs lit up with January 18, 1770 hits—and returned silence for September 1770—that proved our retrieval pipeline had high recall and that the September silence in southern Europe was physically real rather than a search failure.
    3. Allowing for slow mail and other words for the sky. Because news from India, Java, Mauritius, and the Caribbean took two to six months to reach London and Amsterdam, we swept British and Dutch periodicals through mid-1771, and searched VOC Dutch archives for descriptive sky terms (luchtverschijnsel, zuiderlicht, roode/vuurige lucht, bloedrood, compas) rather than just “aurora.”
    4. Checking dates, citations, and page images by eye. Direct scan and convention checks caught five classic historical traps: (a) St. Petersburg Old Style dates (+11 days, shifting Euler’s Sept O.S. table to Sept 12–Oct 11 N.S.); (b) Rubenson’s noon-to-noon astronomical day (meaning Norrbärke “10 m.” is civil morning Sept 11, and Lund OCR “3,30” is 8:30 PM); (c) publication lag (Swedish Academy 1770 printed the 1769 comet; Duhamel’s “stationary needle” belonged to Dec 1770 and his Sept 6 storm was 1771); (d) false-positive “Aurora” homonyms (the lost British frigate HMS Aurora dominated late-1770/1771 British and VOC hits); and (e) OCR drift (inspecting Cotte’s original p. 26 scan corrected “15 à 20” to “15 & 20 minutes” and pinned Paulian’s Nîmes entry to pp. 211–212).

    Where to look next

    This sweep closes most open digitized European academy volumes and Dutch Asian archives, leaving four sharply defined manuscript or paywalled targets that could push the 1770 reconstruction even further:

    1. Nicolaus von Béguelin’s 1770 Berlin Academy memoir. Harvard’s digitized run of Nouveaux mémoires de l’Académie Royale des Sciences et Belles-Lettres skips Année MDCCLXX (Berlin, 1772, pp. 75–96). Béguelin kept daily weather, aurora, and magnetic-needle notes (confirmed from his 1771 volume); reading his actual 1770 pages would test whether Berlin recorded a numerical compass deflection on Sept 9 or Sept 17 alongside Cotte’s Montmorency reading.
    2. Giuseppe Toaldo’s Padua register (+48.3° MLAT). Toaldo’s first edition (dated 20 Sept 1770) stops at winter 1769–70, and the modern catalog of his 1766–1797 Padua manuscript logs (Domínguez-Castro et al., SWSC 6, A21, 2016) sits behind a DataDome check. Verifying whether Padua logged clear skies or an aurora on Sept 17 would pair directly with Paulian’s Nîmes (+48.0°) sightline.
    3. Unpublished Swedish observatory weather logs (Sept 11–18). Rubenson (1879) extracted aurora sightings from manuscript registers at Stockholm (MS C), Uppsala (Mt C), and Lund (ML C; Ld C), showing zero Swedish auroras between Sept 11 and Sept 18. Checking the underlying sky-cover columns in the Royal Swedish Academy archives would establish whether Sweden was clouded out on Sept 17–18 or genuinely quiet on Sept 18 while Trondhjem (+65.7°) glowed.
    4. 1770 British East Indiaman, Royal Navy, and Moravian logs. No 1770 ship logs outside HMS Endeavour have been systematically read for Sept 10–19. British Library India Office logbooks (IOR/L/MAR/B) for ships crossing the Indian Ocean and South Atlantic in September 1770, Royal Navy captains’ logs (TNA ADM 51), and Moravian mission diaries in Greenland and Labrador remain the best chance of adding a second Southern Hemisphere or North Atlantic sighting.7

At Nîmes (43.84°N, 4.36°E) on 1770-09-17 20:30 local apparent solar time (~20:07 UTC, JD 2367799.338), Capella stood at Alt 9.2°, Az 31.7° (NNE). Using Earth radius 6,371 km and a standard 400 km red-oxygen (630.0 nm) emission altitude yields a ground distance of 12.6° (54.1°N, 15.6°E = +56.0° MLAT in the 1770 GUFM1 dipole).
