Mauritius · April 1615

The dodos in a ship’s journal

In April 1615, Dutch sailors went ashore on Mauritius and brought dodos back to their ship. Two entries in their journal fill a gap in the short written history of this extinct, flightless bird.

The problem

How did a painter in India know what a dodo looked like? The dodo was a large, flightless bird whose only wild home was Mauritius, an island east of Madagascar. It died out in the seventeenth century. Few people who saw one left a drawing. One of the most lifelike surviving pictures comes from India, thousands of miles away.

The artist usually credited with the dodo is Mansur. He painted animals for Jahangir, the Mughal emperor who ruled much of South Asia from 1605 to 1627. The bird now appears among parrots and other birds on an album page. Someone assembled that page later from several pictures. Even the date specialists give the dodo itself, around 1615, is an estimate.1

How the search worked

Dutch East India Company ships stopped at Mauritius for fresh water and food. Their officers kept journals of winds, repairs, letters and provisions. An entry about filling water casks could mention an animal in passing.

Using GLOBALISE’s machine transcriptions, the agents searched for old spellings of the bird’s name and for passages about hunting or taking on provisions. Each method found things the other could miss. A search by meaning missed a known 1629 dodo reference buried on a page about crew changes. A spelling search recovered it. The agents then compared the promising words with the handwriting and the dates with published lists of sightings.2

What turned up

On 17 April 1615, hunters returned to the Dutch ship Wapen van Amsterdam with tortoises and dodos. Four days later, the crew fetched water and caught dodos again. Both entries fit on one page. Turn the leaf and a third mention appears in the ship’s account of the provisions it gathered before sailing on.

These entries add two days to the short list of occasions when someone actually recorded encountering a dodo in the wild. A widely used chronology goes from 1611 to 1616 without this stop. The ship’s voyage was already known, and a rhymed account was in print. The apparent addition to dodo history is the dated evidence in the prose journal.3

Text beside the manuscript
17 April 1615The hunters come back

“Hunters went out and brought some tortoises and dodos aboard.”

“Jagers vuyt die sommighe schiltpadden & dodersen aen boort brochten”

The ship had sent its small boat to carry a letter to two other ships. The writer gives the hunters’ return just a few words at the end of the day’s entry.

21 April 1615A second catch

“On the 21st, fetched water again and caught many tortoises, dodos and some geese and parrots.”

“Den 21 dito wederom water gehaelt ende veel schiltpadden dodeersen ende sommighe gansen & papegayen geuangen”

The writer uses a slightly different spelling here. “Many” clearly applies to the tortoises. The entry leaves the number of dodos open.

30 April 1615What the island provided

“Obtained fresh provisions, including dodos and plentiful land tortoises and sea turtles.”

“goede ververssinge gecregen, soe van dodeersen, landt ende water schiltpadden met meenichte”

As the ship prepares to leave, the writer sums up what the crew obtained at the anchorage. This third mention looks back over the stay. Counting it as another hunt would count the same evidence twice.

The same voyageThe dodo in a sailor’s verse

“There are dodos and partridges,
geese worth praising,
and tortoises there.”

“dodersen en patrysen
gansen weert om te prijsen
en schiltpadden daer syn”

Johan Both Volkertsz put the voyage into rhyme. His poem drew on the ship’s journal and was published in a modern edition in 1971. It preserves the name in another form of writing about the same voyage.

November 1638What was a “field-hen”?

“Cattle, geese, field-hens, ducks, pigeons.”

“koebeesten gansz velthoenderen eenden duijven”

In a December letter, the Dutch commander Cornelis Gooyer described sailing around Mauritius the previous month. A French edition in 1890 rendered “field-hens” as “partridges.” Other Dutch visitors used a similar word for the red rail, another flightless Mauritian bird that later died out. Gooyer gives too little detail for a certain identification.

A second bird hidden in translation

The last manuscript above comes from the first year of Dutch settlement on Mauritius. Its author lists “field-hens” between geese and ducks. A reader of the later French translation would find partridges there. Someone searching only that edition for a red rail could pass straight over the passage.

The original wording makes this a possible sighting of the red rail in 1638. The same search that recovered a date for the dodo also turned up a place where one translated word may have obscured another animal.4

Did a bird reach the painter?

The 1615 journal follows the hunters as far as their ship. Other travellers left clues closer to India. A Portuguese expedition caught a young bird on Mauritius the following year. An English traveller later recalled seeing two dodos in Surat. Following the dates and places shows where a bird can be traced and where its trail breaks off.5

Where the records place the birds

A young bird caught on Mauritius

In 1616 Manuel de Almeida, a Portuguese Jesuit priest, described catching what he called a young ostrich. The place and description have led historians to identify it as a dodo. Almeida leaves its fate open. Read the French edition, p. 117.

A ship returns to Goa

After visiting Madagascar, one ship from Almeida’s expedition reached this port on India’s west coast on 17 September 1616. The arrival account names a Malagasy passenger presented to the Portuguese viceroy. Whether the bird boarded either ship or survived the journey is unknown. Read the arrival account, p. 208.

Two dodos at an English trading house

English traveller Peter Mundy wrote, “I saw two of them in Suratt howse that were brought from thence.” He meant Mauritius. Mundy stayed in Surat in 1628–30 and again in 1633–34. He gives neither a date for the sighting nor the name of the person who brought the birds. Read Mundy’s account, pp. 318–19.

What to look for next

A letter saying who received a bird at Surat, or a household entry paying someone to feed or transport it inland, could connect a port sighting to an artist or patron. The bird’s description matters as much as its destination. In 1625, for instance, a Dutch merchant did send a large flightless bird to Jahangir. His letter calls it a cassowary, a different bird that the Dutch also traded.6

The dated catches already tell us something about the dodo on Mauritius. Finding the painter’s model will take a record that follows an individual bird one step further.
