Ceres, South Africa · December 1893 · Wax cylinder

A voice from South Africa, 1893

Quieting a repeating scratch on an unidentified 1893 brown-wax cylinder at UC Santa Barbara revealed what appears to be the earliest known surviving voice recording made in South Africa—thirteen years before the 1906 milestone usually cited. For the first time, the scrubbed audio identifies the speaker, exact date, town, and house, and traces the cylinder back to the London agent who recorded Alfred, Lord Tennyson.

A Victorian minister meets the phonograph

On the evening of Friday, 15 December 1893, an elderly Dutch Reformed minister stepped up to the brass horn of an Edison phonograph in the small Cape Colony town of Ceres, roughly ninety miles northeast of Cape Town. He had never heard a sound recording before. Speaking slowly and deliberately in formal pulpit Dutch, he marveled at the machine—and made a prediction about its future:1

“I have heard much of the so-called phonograph. This evening I heard it for the first time, at the house of Mr. Home at Ceres… I do not doubt for a moment that the telephone will one day be used for the spread of the Word. The Gospel will one day without doubt be preached through the phonograph. The time is fast approaching when this great wonder shall be used among us… to extend His kingdom through the preaching of the Word in an artificial manner.”

Then, like someone signing a letter, he spoke his full name, the exact date, and his office into the wax: “Johan Andrew Stegmann. Fifteen December eighteen hundred and ninety-three. Minister of the Dutch Reformed Church at Ceres.”

Over the next century, the brown-wax cylinder made its way into a private American collection and eventually to the UC Santa Barbara Cylinder Audio Archive, where it was digitized as Cylinder 13010. By then, surface hiss and a repeating vertical scratch had swallowed the consonants in his closing signature. The archive’s cataloguer caught the year 1893 and made a reasonable guess at the garbled name—“Johan Andrew Dechman?”—while leaving the exact date, the host’s name, and the church unreadable.

Putting the right biography to the voice

Once the repeating scratch is lowered, every syllable of the closing signature comes into focus: Johan Andrew Stegmann, 15 December 1893, Predikant der Nederduitse Gereformeerde Kerk te Ceres. South African church histories immediately identify him. Born around April 1825 in Cape Town and named in honor of his brother-in-law, the prominent Scottish-born clergyman Andrew Murray Sr., Ds. Johan Andrew Stegmann served as the very first resident minister of the Ceres Dutch Reformed congregation from 1857 until his retirement in 1883. He was sixty-eight years old when he spoke into the horn in December 1893, and lived until 5 January 1908, dying at Sea Point in Cape Town.2

Checking the genealogy closely also prevented an easy mistake. Cape Town archives list a near-contemporary namesake, Johannes Augustus Stegmann (born 31 October 1819, died 17 August 1894), from another branch of the same extended Cape family. Listening at 0.70× speed confirms the speaker says Andrew—not Augustus—and church chronicles confirm it was Johan Andrew Stegmann (1825–1908) who spent twenty-six years leading the Ceres parish.

Even the brief English message tacked onto the last thirty-five seconds of blank wax fits the calendar. After Stegmann finishes, a jovial English-speaking man jokes into the horn, “Well now, Mr. Phonograph… This is Sunday afternoon, a jolly good way of spending it, isn’t it? Here all the girls come down in their nightgowns—I mean to say in their dressing gowns…” In 1893, 15 December fell on a Friday. Two days later, on Sunday afternoon, 17 December, someone in the same household appears to have used up the final half-minute of wax.

How an Edison agent from London ended up in Ceres

Why was a working Edison brown-wax phonograph sitting in a parlor in rural Cape Colony in December 1893—barely five years after Edison introduced wax cylinders in New Jersey? The answer was sitting right next door in UCSB’s catalogue, on Cylinder 13009 (Set 197-01).

UCSB’s note on Cylinder 13009 remarked that an English speaker and a subsequent Afrikaans speaker both seemed to address a “Mister Steytler.” Listening to the scrubbed English half reveals the missing place name: the speaker plans to take the train to Cape Town on Monday night to meet arriving relatives at the docks, hoping “that they’ll arrive at Ceres at the end of the week.”3

In the history of early recorded sound, there is only one “Mister Steytler” linked to both Edison phonographs and Ceres, South Africa: Charles Rhenius Cruywagen Steytler (c. 1864 – 10 September 1919). Born into a prominent Cape Colony family, Steytler went to London in the late 1880s and joined the small team of recording agents working for Colonel George Edward Gouraud, Thomas Edison’s exclusive European representative. In May 1890, Steytler traveled to Farringford on the Isle of Wight to record the eighty-year-old Poet Laureate, Alfred, Lord Tennyson, reciting “The Charge of the Light Brigade” onto Edison wax cylinders. By April 1891 he was touring British theaters lecturing on “Edison’s Latest and Most Perfected Phonograph.”

Soon afterward, Steytler returned home to the Cape—and brought Edison’s technology with him. One final archival record locks the entire story shut. On the 1893 cylinder, Ds. Stegmann says he heard the phonograph “at the house of Mr. Home at Ceres.” Parish marriage registers for St. Andrew’s Anglican Church in Ceres record that on 10 February 1897, Charles Rhenius Cruywagen Steytler married Margaret Henrietta Home—daughter of the Home family of Ceres. On that Friday evening in December 1893, the man who had recorded Tennyson in England was demonstrating his brown-wax phonograph in the parlor of his future in-laws.3

How two unidentified catalogue records unlocked each other

Both cylinders sit side by side in the UC Santa Barbara Cylinder Audio Archive as Set 197-01 and Set 197-02, acquired together from the Don Hill Collection around 2000. On its own, neither record revealed where it was made. Put together, they point to the same small South African town—and the same Victorian recording pioneer.

“Mister Steytler! Our minister seems a dear man…”

Unlike Stegmann’s formal 1893 Cape Dutch, this passage is unmistakably spoken Afrikaans—praising a newly arrived Ceres minister (possibly Ds. R. D. McDonald, installed in 1911).

Cylinder 13009 · English opening (0:00–1:02)

“Arrive at Ceres at the end of the week”

“This is the first time I’ve ever spoken into a gramophone, and I don’t quite believe that my voice will ever come out again! … I’m going to Cape Town on Monday night, and I hope to be at the docks at 8 o’clock on Tuesday morning … and that they’ll arrive at Ceres at the end of the week.”

Immediately afterward, the speaker sings “Two Little Sausages” from the London musical The Girls of Gottenberg (May 1907), establishing a firm earliest date for this cylinder.

Cylinder 13011 · Set 197-03 · Catalogue correction

Not Afrikaans—Tyrolean German yodeling

UCSB catalogued Cylinder 13011 as “Male singing in Afrikaans.” Scrubbed at 165.01 RPM, it is actually an Austrian/Tyrolean German yodeling song (“Es zog ein Pater von Österreich… der ging so gern nach Frankreich…”).

Cylinder 13012 · Set 197-04 · 96.60 RPM

Schiller’s “Song of the Bell” & Shelley

Recorded at the same slow early-1890s speed (~100 RPM) as Stegmann’s 1893 cylinder, Cylinder 13012 opens with a speaker announcing “an extract from Schiller’s Song of the Bell, a poem I had to learn by heart when I was a schoolboy,” then switches to Shelley’s “To a Skylark,” laughter, and a Spanish sign-off.

What makes this find historically significant—and what still needs checking

It helps to be precise about what these cylinders are—and what they are not. Cylinder 13010 is not spoken in modern vernacular Afrikaans, despite UCSB’s catalogue tag. Like most educated Dutch Reformed clergy of his generation, Ds. Stegmann used formal Cape Dutch (Hoog-Hollands) for serious public reflections, spoken with a South African cadence. Vernacular spoken Afrikaans arrives on the companion cylinder, Cylinder 13009, where a local speaker addresses “Mister Steytler” in unmistakable conversational Afrikaans (“Ons predikant lyk my liewe man… ons gemeente hoog tevrede is met hom”). Because Cylinder 13009 also quotes a song from the May 1907 London musical The Girls of Gottenberg, that recording dates to 1907 or later—possibly around 1911, when Ceres welcomed a new minister, Ds. R. D. McDonald, after the previous pastor drowned in December 1910.4

Even with that distinction kept sharp, the 15 December 1893 recording is remarkable. Standard South African sound histories place the earliest commercial Afrikaans recording in July 1906—made overseas in London by the touring Springbok rugby team (“Springboks Conversing in the Taal,” Gramophone Concert Record GC 4608)—and the earliest major ethnographic cylinder collections made inside Southern Africa (such as Dorothea Bleek’s and Percival Kirby’s recordings) from roughly 1905 onward. An explicitly dated, localized, identified recording made in Ceres in December 1893 sits thirteen years earlier, and directly links the founding generation of Edison’s London phonograph circle to rural South Africa.4

Three checks still belong on the to-do list before calling this the absolute earliest surviving South African recording:

  • Check with South African sound historians and UCSB Special Collections: Confirm with specialists (such as Schalk D. van der Merwe, Patrick Feaster, and UCSB’s cylinder curators) whether any earlier 1889–1893 colonial Cape recordings survive in private hands or uncatalogued museum boxes, and submit the Set 197 identifications and the Cylinder 13011 language correction to UCSB.
  • Search 1891–1894 Cape newspapers and De Kerkbode: Contemporary Cape Town or Western Cape press notices of C. R. C. Steytler’s phonograph demonstrations—or a church note mentioning Ds. Stegmann speaking into the machine in Ceres—would independently corroborate the evening of 15 December 1893.
  • Compare the English coda voice against Tennyson’s 1890 cylinders: It is plausible that the jovial English speaker on the final thirty-five seconds of Cylinder 13010 is C. R. C. Steytler himself (or a member of the Home family). Comparing his voice against any surviving spoken announcement by Steytler on Gouraud’s 1890 British cylinders could test that possibility directly.

Methodology: How to quiet a 133-year-old scratch without inventing speech

Anyone trying to decipher an old wax cylinder faces a trap. Modern AI “speech enhancement” tools can make a noisy tape sound startlingly crisp—by guessing what syllables ought to be there and generating new speech from scratch. For historical work, that is fatal. A generative denoiser can easily turn a faint Stegmann into a crystal-clear Dechman (or vice versa), leaving the researcher with fabricated audio that looks clean but cannot be trusted as evidence.5

Instead, our pipeline relies on a simple physical fact about wax cylinders—and uses zero generative AI on the audio itself. A brown-wax cylinder spins on a brass shaft at a steady speed (typically between 95 and 165 revolutions per minute). Every scratch, mold spot, or crack on the wax strikes the playback stylus at the exact same point in every turn. A human voice, by contrast, never repeats the same syllable at the exact same millisecond on every turn of the cylinder. That physical difference lets us separate wax noise from speech deterministically:

  1. Step 1 — Light click suppression without smearing consonants: We decode the archive transfer to uncompressed 24 kHz mono audio and run a gentle median de-click filter (ffmpeg adeclick) to knock down sharp isolated pops while keeping the full acoustic speech band intact (150–7,800 Hz).
  2. Step 2 — Automatic rotation-speed lock: Rather than guessing the playback speed, a Python script isolates the high-frequency surface hiss (3.5–8 kHz, where wax noise dominates over early acoustic voices), computes its amplitude envelope, and correlates that envelope against itself across lags from 0.30 to 1.30 seconds. A sharp spike appears at the cylinder’s true physical revolution period T—on Cylinder 13010, exactly 0.600 seconds (100.0 RPM, r = +0.44), with a clean second harmonic at two turns (1.20 s).
  3. Step 3 — Full-band phase-locked spectral scrubbing (150–7,800 Hz): We convert the audio into a time-frequency spectrogram (Short-Time Fourier Transform) and compare every tiny slice of sound at time t against the exact same angular position on the wax one, two, and three turns earlier and later (t ± kT). Frequencies that stay constant across neighboring turns belong to the repeating wax defect; frequencies that appear on only one turn belong to the speaker. A smooth Wiener-style gain mask lowers only the repeating noise profile while leaving the original acoustic phase completely untouched. Nothing is synthesized or hallucinated.
  4. Step 4 — Direct listening, slowed verification, and archival triangulation: Instead of trusting automatic speech-to-text on historical accents and 19th-century Dutch, we listen directly to targeted excerpts—and slow key passages to 0.70× speed (preserving pitch) so consonant clusters in names and place-names can be checked syllable by syllable. Once hard anchors emerge (Johan Andrew Stegmann + 15 December 1893 + Mr. Home at Ceres + Mister Steytler), we verify every candidate across independent church histories, parish marriage registers, and sibling cylinders in the same archival box.

Scaling up across the remaining unidentified cylinders

The Ceres box was only one of eight targets in our first test run. The same automated scrubber also recovered UCSB’s missing bracketed words on a rare Jèrriais (Jersey Norman French) recording—revealing the announcement “Conversation between two farmers in the Island of Jersey during the potato season — Edison Record” on Cylinder 12884—and pulled crisp names, dates, and street addresses out of recordings from 1897 New York, 1900 Ireland, and early-20th-century Austria.6

More than a hundred additional unidentified home wax cylinders sit digitized in the UCSB archive alone, alongside thousands more in the Library of Congress, the British Library, and regional sound archives worldwide. Because rotation-locked scrubbing runs locally in a few seconds per cylinder and never fabricates speech, pairing it with systematic archival verification turns noisy “undecipherable” catalog entries into searchable historical evidence—one turn of the wax at a time.
