Buru, 1738 · Earthquakes

The day the sea ran up the rivers

In 1738, people on an Indonesian island fled uphill as seawater rushed inland. A Dutch official described it twice. His letters preserve evidence of a tsunami missing from modern catalogues.

Compare the two letters ↓

A morning on Buru

At about eleven on 14 September 1738, the ground shook on Buru, an island in what is now eastern Indonesia. Then the sea came up the rivers so fast that the moat around the Dutch trading post filled almost at once. People ran uphill. The next day, Carel Gustaeff Gledstein, the Dutch East India Company official stationed there, sent an account to his superior on the island of Ambon.1

Some of what Gledstein knew came from people around him. The headman of Masarete reported that an older man had drowned. A sergeant and an assistant surgeon examined the body. The letter preserves the headman’s report at second hand. The man who died goes unnamed in this passage.2

Language of the excerpts
Which entry would you put in an earthquake catalogue?

The first letter gives four. The later letter gives three. An entry can preserve both readings while leaving the precise count open.

Both letters support this entry. The earlier one also tells us where the water went, who fled, and how word of a death reached the writer.

Look at who sent the letters. Both came from Gledstein. A second local account could tell us what someone else saw.

How the search worked

The Company sent copies of letters and reports back to the Netherlands. Today the GLOBALISE project makes machine transcriptions of those handwritten pages searchable. Research agents looked for old Dutch words for shaking ground and rising water, then checked the dates, places and wording in the manuscript images. Reading the heading on each letter revealed that one official had written both.3

What the letters add

A tsunami is a series of waves that forms when a large amount of water is suddenly displaced. The timing of the shaking and the repeated rushes of seawater make a strong case that one reached Buru. Long before instruments recorded these waves, people kept track of where the water went. Their accounts can extend the history of a particular coast by centuries. The search found no entry for these waves on this date in the Indonesian and international catalogues it checked. A separate entry mentions earthquake damage to a church on Buru in 1739. Whether the same earthquake damaged the church remains an open question.4

Where else did the water reach?

Gledstein gave no wave height, and his letters alone cannot locate the earthquake offshore. A ship’s log or a daily register kept at another post could record the same morning from a different stretch of coast. Even a few words about when the sea arrived, or where it stayed calm, would help establish how far the waves travelled.
