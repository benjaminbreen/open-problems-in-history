Washington, D.C. · March 1902

Did Langley ever try Bell’s kite designs?

Samuel Pierpont Langley, an astronomer who ran the Smithsonian Institution, had flown a steam-powered model airplane. Now he was building one large enough to carry a pilot. Alexander Graham Bell, who had patented the telephone in 1876, had spent years building kites. After the two men tried one of Langley’s kites together, Bell suggested two ways to rebuild it. A few pencilled lines in a notebook preserve his advice.

A kite that might right itself

On March 10, 1902, Langley and Bell took a boat to Analostan Island in the Potomac River at Washington, D.C. They had come to try a kite with a six-sided frame and a curious tail. As Bell understood it, if the kite tilted, a weight would slide along a rod and adjust the tail to bring the kite level again. For anyone hoping to put a person in the air, it mattered whether a flying machine could recover its balance.

That afternoon there was hardly any wind. Bell ran with the string, then let go when he saw that Langley’s kite would come down hard. In his journal he suggested trying again from the top of a pole, where Langley could watch how the kite behaved without smashing it.1

What the notebook adds to Bell’s account

AI agents found another account among Langley’s papers. Partway down a page of shorthand, the writer switched to ordinary handwriting and wrote “Mr Bell suggested”. The kite’s frame formed a single open compartment, called a cell. Bell proposed adding a second cell directly behind it. His other idea was to add one cell on each side, then move the middle one back about six inches.2

How the agents identified the kite

The Smithsonian catalogues this notebook as undated. Even with Bell’s name, which kite did the writer mean? Page 15 gives two figures, 930 grams and 50 × 25 centimetres. Bell copied into his journal a letter Langley sent him on March 11 about the previous day’s trial. The kite weighed 930 grams with its balancing weight, Langley wrote, and each of its twelve panels measured about 50 × 25 centimetres. Those figures tie the notebook passage to the March 10 outing.3

Why the shorthand is still unread

The kite passage turned up in a search for something harder to read. The Smithsonian holds 37 notebooks from Langley’s papers, much of their content written in shorthand, a way of taking rapid notes by using strokes and curves to stand for sounds or whole words. AI agents sampled 1,108 of the collection’s 7,248 scans. They also ran handwriting recognition over every page of four notebooks to look for names and phrases in ordinary script. The software sometimes turned shorthand strokes into plausible English words, so the agents checked promising passages against the page images.4

In another notebook, the words “towed by a steamship” were legible. Just before them was a single downward stroke. The agents compared it with two shorthand manuals of the period. In one system a heavy stroke of that shape could mean “all”, while a lighter one could mean “to”. The pencil pressure varied across the page. Neither the neighboring pages nor a search of Langley’s published account supplied the same sentence in ordinary writing. The agents could identify the kite and Bell’s advice, but they could not reliably transcribe a continuous passage of shorthand.5

Did either design get into the air?

The agents found no record of a flight with either arrangement. One place to look is the Smithsonian’s diary of Langley’s experimental work for the U.S. Army in 1901–02. An entry after March 10 describing two cells in line, or three across with the middle one set back, would be worth comparing with dated workshop drawings. An account of a launch with that arrangement could establish that someone actually tried it.6

The shorthand presents a different opening. A specialist could start with a dated notebook entry and a letter about the same experiment. If several consecutive words in the letter fit the strokes on the page, a second reader could test that reading independently. That would give researchers a way to check what the notebooks say about experiments for which no letter survives.
