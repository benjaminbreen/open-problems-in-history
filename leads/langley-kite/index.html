Potomac River · March 1902

Did Langley’s team test either of Alexander Graham Bell’s kite designs?

On March 10, 1902, Samuel Langley and Alexander Graham Bell went to an island in the Potomac to fly a kite. There was so little wind that Bell ran with the string, then let go to keep Langley’s kite from smashing into the ground. A notebook from the outing records Bell suggesting two ways to rebuild it. A later flight record could tell us whether his advice changed what Langley’s team flew.

What did Bell suggest?

The kite had a cell, an open section that caught the wind. Bell proposed putting a second cell behind the first. Or the team could add one on either side and move the original cell back about six inches. The proposal starts halfway down one notebook page and ends at the top of the next.

Did either design fly?

The notebook records a suggestion. A dated workshop drawing with two cells in line or three cells side by side would show that someone worked up one of the designs. A flight log, photograph or letter describing a test of that arrangement would take the story a step further. The place to look is in the team’s papers in the weeks after March 10.

Methodology ¶

The Smithsonian catalogues this notebook as undated. Pages 14 and 15 switch from shorthand to ordinary handwriting. Page 15 gives a weight of 930 grams and measurements of 50 by 25 centimetres. The same figures appear in Langley’s March 11 letter to Bell about the previous day’s trial, copied into Bell’s journal on pages 631–634. That match places the notebook passage at a particular outing. Whether the team acted on Bell’s advice remains open.
