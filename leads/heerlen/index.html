Heerlen, Netherlands · Object 04433

What game wore down this stone?

A piece of limestone from the Roman town beneath Heerlen has a smooth patch beside one diagonal cut. In 2026, researchers proposed that players wore it down in a game where one side tried to trap the other. Could three in a row leave the same trace?

What can a worn line tell us?

No surviving Roman text describes a blocking game. If the people of Heerlen played one, this little stone preserves a part of everyday life their writers missed. But three in a row also sent counters around the same corner. The wear tells us where pieces may have travelled. What the players were trying to do is still an open question.

A next experiment would give people both games, record their moves, and ask someone who never saw them play to identify the rules from the tracks.

The blind test pairs two deliberately similar cases from the authors’ replication archive, version 1.0. One uses three counters per player. The other starts with two hares and three dogs, hares first. The four-dog game is a separate published candidate. Dogs and hares are names borrowed from later games. The stone preserves no counters or names for the pieces. The one-move puzzle starts just before the final slide in its first deposited game (trial 0). The archive contains individual trial logs, raw counts and adjusted percentages. Percentages count moves along edges. Initial placements contribute zero. All maps use the same line-width scale. The diagonal cutoff was the first screen. The authors also examined the centre, the adjacent edges and how much of the board saw play.
