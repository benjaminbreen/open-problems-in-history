Methods

This site is currently a prototype. Most of the questions on it are ones I chose based on my own research and on conversations with other historians. It also includes several questions suggested directly by GPT-6 Pro and Claude Opus 5.5, after I prompted these models to “cast a wide net” across historiography and digitized archives in search of important open questions that might prove tractable to collaborative teams of historians and interested amateurs using AI tools for data mining and quantitative analysis at scale. As of October 2026, I am crowdsourcing additional questions from colleagues and members of the public, and you can suggest one here. New questions will be added to the site and then rated by professional historians to arrive at a final set of problems.

The individual pages for each open question were overseen by me (Benjamin Breen), but they are mostly the product of research into the available sources conducted in October 2026 by Claude Opus 5.5. I edited and checked all of the text and verified every link to a citation. If you would like to make a correction or suggest an improvement, you can open an issue here or email me.

To make clear which parts of this site are human-written and which were drafted by an AI model and then edited by a human (me!), I have added attribution labels like the one below. For more on the project as a whole, see the About page.

How the questions are ranked

Which investigation is most likely to teach us something consequential? On November 19, 2026, Claude Opus 5.5 (low reasoning effort), Gemini 4 Hydrogen (medium reasoning effort), GPT-6, and GPT-6.2 Astra (high reasoning effort) each judged the same 72 questions. Each received the same short account of the available evidence and the same instructions. The judges reported no prior exposure to rankings of these questions.

First, each judge estimated the chance of reaching an answer another historian could verify, using today’s tools and public digital sources. A well-supported negative answer counts. Then, assuming the question had been answered, the judge assessed two things on a scale of 1 to 10. How much would the answer change what historians know? And what other research would it make possible?

Overall potential multiplies each judge’s estimate of the chance of a convincing answer by the historical contribution that answer would make. Suppose a judge gives one question a contribution of 10 out of 10 but only a 5% chance of an answer. It scores 0.5. A question with a contribution of 7 and a 70% chance scores 4.9, so it ranks higher on that ballot. The AI ranking gives each ballot equal weight. Each GPT run counts as a separate ballot. The current list uses the November 19 scores until the next panel returns its ballots. Where the wording changed, the score table shows the question the panel saw. The panel rated Aceh and Padang separately. The new question about Sumatra’s tsunami history awaits a fresh rating.

Choose Further research to see which answers could open up the most work for others. This ranking multiplies the same chance estimates by each judge’s assessment of that follow-on value.

The human column uses the same calculation across approved human ballots from November 20. Earlier ratings carry forward for questions whose briefs are identical. James Pawpaw’s remedy now appears as a lead within the Sloane question. Those raters see a fuller account of the evidence and one suggested investigation for each question. You can rate any questions you know. Each question shows its number of human raters. A dash means it has no rating in that column. Equal scores share a rank. A difference of a few hundredths can change the order, especially with a small panel.
