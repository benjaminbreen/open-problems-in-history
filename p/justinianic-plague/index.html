How many people did the Justinianic Plague kill, and did it change late antique society?

Ancient DNA confirms Yersinia pestis caused the First Pandemic (541–c.750), but estimates of its mortality and long-term consequences range from catastrophic to modest.

Disease · Mediterranean · 541–750science

Could use help from geneticists, epidemiologists and archaeologists.

Why it matters

Maximalist readings make the pandemic a cause of the end of antiquity: depopulation weakening the eastern Roman empire before the Arab conquests, contraction of cities and trade, and the rural and fiscal transformations of the seventh century. Minimalist readings remove that causal link and return explanatory weight to war, climate and political decisions. The answer also shapes comparisons with the Black Death and with modern pandemics, and the use of plague as a general explanation in historical narratives.

Why it is open

Narrative sources (Procopius, John of Ephesus, Evagrius, Gregory of Tours) give vivid but rhetorically shaped figures for a few cities, and there are almost no serial demographic records. Documentary proxies such as papyri, legislation, coin output, inscriptions and building activity can be read as showing disruption or continuity depending on the baseline chosen. Ancient DNA proves presence of the pathogen at particular sites but not mortality rates, and pollen and settlement data are often too coarse in date to isolate the 540s from other causes.

What would count as a solution

A region-by-region database of dated proxies (papyri, legislation, coin issues, dated inscriptions, burials with plague DNA, high-resolution pollen cores, settlement and building chronologies) showing, with stated uncertainties, whether measurable discontinuities cluster at the documented plague waves more than expected from a baseline of other shocks, yielding bounded mortality estimates for named regions.

Potential approach

  • Build on the source surveys of (1969) and (2004), the essays in (2007), and the maximalist synthesis of (2017), read against the minimalist critique of (2019) and the replies by (2021) and (2020). (2018) is a warning about reusing older compilations of plague mentions without returning to the sources.
  • Extract and date every reference to plague waves in narrative and documentary sources, then count dated papyri from the , inscriptions, laws and coin issues per year and region. The main decision is how to separate a genuine fall in documentation from the uneven survival of sources, so every series needs an explicit pre-541 baseline.
  • Two readings compete: a demographic catastrophe that reshaped the empire, and a serious but locally bounded epidemic. Event-study comparisons around each wave would tell them apart: a maximalist case predicts synchronous breaks across regions and source types, a minimalist one scattered or absent breaks.
  • Align these series with pathogen genomes from (2013), (2014) and (2019), with pollen records in , and with site studies such as (2019) and (2026). Epidemic models like (2020), which found the reported Constantinople mortality and duration hard to reconcile, can test whether source claims are internally plausible.
  • A dated, region-by-region register of attested outbreaks and documentary series would be useful even if mortality stays unresolved. Any death toll will remain a wide range that differs by region rather than a single imperial figure.
  • Late antique historians, papyrologists and numismatists should review the coding and any agent-produced counts. Confirmation depends on ancient-DNA laboratories screening more burials across the Mediterranean and Near East, and on palynologists producing well-dated, high-resolution cores.

Who can help

geneticists
Align the pathogen genomes from Harbeck (2013), Wagner (2014) and Keller (2019) with dated burials, so each site with plague DNA enters the regional register with its date range.
epidemiologists
Test, following White (2020), whether the mortality and duration reported for each city are internally plausible under epidemic models, and state which source claims fail.
archaeologists
Supply dated settlement, building and pollen chronologies by region, with explicit pre-541 baselines, so event-study comparisons can test for breaks at each plague wave.
