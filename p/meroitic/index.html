Can the Meroitic language be translated beyond its funerary formulae?

Meroitic script has been readable since Griffith's work of 1909–1911, but outside formulaic texts the language of the Kingdom of Kush is still largely untranslated.

Languages & scripts · Africa · 300 BCE–450 CEdigitizationdecipherment

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers, archaeologists and archivists and librarians.

Why it matters

Meroitic is the oldest written language of sub-Saharan Africa and the administrative and royal language of Kush for some seven centuries. The long royal stelae, temple texts and chronicles almost certainly record wars with Rome, succession and religious practice from a Kushite point of view; at present historians rely on Greek, Latin and Egyptian sources for that history. A secure classification would also date the spread of Northern East Sudanic languages along the Nile, which bears on Nubian linguistic and population history.

Why it is open

The script's sound values were recovered from Egyptian names, but there is no substantial bilingual text, and the language has no close, well-documented descendant. Most of the corpus consists of funerary inscriptions with predictable formulae, so the vocabulary securely understood is small, by most accounts on the order of a hundred words, and grammar is reconstructed mainly from those formulae. Classification is disputed: Rilly argues for the Northern East Sudanic branch with Nubian, Nara, Taman and Nyima, while and Lipiński have argued for an Afroasiatic affiliation.

What would count as a solution

A lexicon and grammar, fixed before testing, yield translations of held-out non-funerary passages (for example sections of royal stelae or temple texts) that independent specialists judge consistent with their archaeological, iconographic and historical context, and that correctly predict content later confirmed by newly found or newly published inscriptions.

Potential approach

  • Since (1911) read the script, progress has come from the formulae and from comparison. The corpus of et al. (2000), (2007) on the lexicon and grammar, (2010) on the language family, and (2012), written with Alex de Voogt, are the base, with (2020) showing how verbal morphology can be recovered through Nubian.
  • The REM texts and inscriptions published since 2000 need to become a lemmatized, machine-readable corpus with find context, object type and date. Segmentation is the decision that matters most, since the word divider is used inconsistently, and every split the corpus adopts should be traceable to an editor.
  • Two classifications compete: the Northern East Sudanic affiliation argued in Rilly (2010), and the Afroasiatic reading of the phonology in (2015). Each predicts different cognates for the basic vocabulary, and (2020) bears on how secure the East Sudanic family itself is, so the corpus can score both against the same held-out vocabulary.
  • Reconstructed Proto-Northern East Sudanic and the Old Nubian lexicon of (1996) can then generate constrained translation hypotheses, tested on passages whose context predicts part of the content: offering scenes, lists of places, and military iconography as on the .
  • A useful intermediate result is a graded glossary that separates secure, probable and conjectural meanings for every recurring word outside the funerary formulae. Royal and historical texts will likely stay partly readable, with the meaning of many passages given as a range of candidates rather than a single translation.
  • This depends on Nubian and Nilo-Saharan linguists reviewing every proposal, and on excavation missions and museums sharing post-2000 inscriptions and photographs, a need sharpened by reported damage to collections in Khartoum during the war that began in 2023.

Who can help

philologists and epigraphers
Compile a graded glossary of recurring words outside the funerary formulae, separating secure, probable and conjectural meanings, and test translation hypotheses drawn from Old Nubian against the Hamadab stela.
archaeologists
Supply find context, object type and date for REM inscriptions and texts published since 2000, so that passages can be read against their offering scenes, place lists and iconography.
archivists and librarians
Turn the scanned REM volumes on Persée and inscriptions published since 2000 into a lemmatized, machine-readable corpus, recording which editor made each word segmentation.

    F. Ll. Griffith, Karanòg: The Meroitic Inscriptions of Shablûl and Karanòg, Eckley B. Coxe Junior Expedition to Nubia 6.     Bruce G. Trigger, Spoken and Written Meroitic: A Note on the Terminal Formulae in Meroitic Funerary Inscriptions from Armenna West, Journal of Egyptian Archaeology 53: 166.Used variation in funerary formulae to infer features of the spoken language behind the written conventions.

    Gerald M. Browne, Old Nubian Dictionary, Corpus Scriptorum Christianorum Orientalium, Subsidia. Leuven: Peeters.Standard lexicon of medieval Old Nubian, the best-attested relative for comparison with Meroitic vocabulary.

    Jean Leclant, André Heyler, Catherine Berger-el Naggar, Claude Carrier and Claude Rilly, Répertoire d'épigraphie méroïtique (REM), tomes I–III, Paris: Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres.Corpus of all Meroitic inscriptions published to that date, numbered REM 0001 to 1278, now openly available on Persée.

    Claude Rilly, Le méroïtique et sa famille linguistique, Afrique et langage 14. Louvain and Paris: Peeters.Comparative study across Nilo-Saharan that places Meroitic in Northern East Sudanic and proposes a reconstruction of the proto-language.
