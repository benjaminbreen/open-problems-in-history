What caused the cocoliztli epidemics of 1545 and 1576 in New Spain?

Two epidemics that Nahua and Spanish observers called cocoliztli killed a large share of central Mexico's Indigenous population, and their cause, whether a native haemorrhagic fever, enteric fever or something else, is disputed.

Disease · Americas · 1545–1581science

Could use help from geneticists, epidemiologists and geoscientists.

Why it matters

The cocoliztli epidemics of 1545–48 and 1576–80 are counted among the largest mortality events in the sixteenth-century collapse of Mexico's Indigenous population, with contemporary and modern estimates of losses ranging widely and in some accounts exceeding the toll of the 1520 smallpox epidemic. Whether they were caused by a pathogen introduced from Europe or Africa, by a native disease released by drought and colonial disruption, or by several agents together changes how the collapse is explained: as an inevitable consequence of contact, or as the product of colonial labour, resettlement and famine acting on susceptible populations.

Why it is open

The clinical descriptions (high fever, bleeding from nose and mouth, jaundice, rapid death, Indigenous victims and few Spanish ones) fit several modern diseases, and sixteenth-century terms like cocoliztli ('pestilence') were generic. Rodolfo Acuña-Soto and colleagues proposed a native rodent-borne haemorrhagic fever amplified by drought, but no such virus has been identified, and RNA viruses rarely survive to be detected in skeletal remains. et al. (2018) recovered Salmonella enterica Paratyphi C from an epidemic cemetery at Teposcolula-Yucundaa, but from one site in the Mixteca Alta and with the acknowledged possibility that other, undetectable pathogens were also present, so the finding has not settled the question for 1545 in central Mexico or for 1576 at all.

What would count as a solution

Pathogen evidence from several independently dated epidemic burials of 1545–48 and 1576–80, in central Mexico as well as Oaxaca, identifies the same agent or combination of agents, and the agent's known epidemiology is consistent with the timing, symptoms, age and ethnic pattern of mortality, and geography recorded in the , parish and .

Potential approach

  • Build on the demographic and documentary work of (1991), (1995) and (1998), the haemorrhagic-fever case made by (2000) and (2000), and the drought argument of (2002). The pictorial annals, read through (1952), and recent readings of the by (2025) show how much the Nahuatl sources still have to give.
  • Extract every report of the 1545 and 1576 epidemics from the of 1579–85, Sahagún's writings, Francisco Hernández, Juan de Torquemada, the and Codex Mexicanus, and the . Each report becomes a record of symptoms, dates, place, mortality claim and who died, with Spanish and Nahuatl testimony kept distinct and the date of writing recorded, since many accounts were set down decades after the events.
  • A georeferenced, source-graded atlas of the two epidemics would be useful on its own terms, showing where the documentary record is dense, where it is silent, and how far later chroniclers copied one another.
  • Set the atlas against the gridded soil-moisture reconstructions of (2016) and against the spread of cattle, wheat, congregación and repartimiento labour. A drought-and-rodent reservoir model, a colonial-disruption model and a contagion-along-routes model predict different geographies and timings of mortality, and the comparison should be reviewed by historians of New Spain before any is preferred.
  • Treat the pathogen as an open question. The Salmonella Paratyphi C genomes from Teposcolula-Yucundaa reported by (2018), with the context in (2012), (2018) and (2025), establish one infection at one site, not the cause of the whole epidemic; mortality totals, too, will stay a wide range.
  • Testing that result depends on and palaeogenomic laboratories: identifying securely dated epidemic burials in Mexico City, Puebla, Oaxaca and elsewhere, and screening them with methods able to recover RNA viruses and parasites as well as bacteria, under protocols agreed with descendant communities.

Who can help

geneticists
Screen securely dated 1545 and 1576 epidemic burials beyond Teposcolula-Yucundaa with methods able to recover RNA viruses and parasites as well as bacteria.
epidemiologists
State what the drought-and-rodent, colonial-disruption and contagion-along-routes models predict for the timing, geography and victims of mortality, and test them against the documentary atlas.
geoscientists
Compare the Stahle gridded soil-moisture reconstructions with dated, georeferenced epidemic reports for 1545 and 1576, and state where drought preceded mortality.
