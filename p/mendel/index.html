Were Mendel's pea-hybrid data too close to expectation, and if so, why?

Fisher showed in 1936 that Mendel's ratios fit theory improbably well; a century of explanations has not produced documentary evidence of how the counts were made.

Science & knowledge · Europe · 1856–1866forensics

Why it matters

's paper is the founding document of genetics and a standard case in teaching about scientific integrity, so whether his numbers were selected, adjusted, unconsciously biased or simply lucky affects how the episode is taught and how historians read his intentions. The answer also bears on what Mendel set out to do, whether he tested a pre-formed theory or reported plant-breeding experiments, and on the reception of his work by Nägeli and the rediscoverers of 1900.

Why it is open

's chi-square analysis found an overall fit with a probability of roughly 7 in 100,000 and also argued that 's 2:1 ratios in the progeny tests should have been biased toward 1.7:1. Mendel's experimental notebooks do not survive, and many of his papers are reported to have been burned after his death in 1884, so only the published paper and a handful of letters to Nägeli constrain explanations. Proposed solutions (a too-eager assistant, sequential stopping, selective reporting, a different sampling model, botanical factors such as pollen tetrads) are each compatible with the summary numbers and therefore hard to discriminate.

What would count as a solution

Either discovery of primary records (field notes, seed counts, or correspondence) that show how collected and selected his counts, or a statistical analysis that specifies a historically documented procedure and shows that it reproduces both 's goodness-of-fit anomaly and the 2:1 progeny-test anomaly at conventional significance, while competing procedures do not.

Potential approach

  • Begin from the statistical literature that already exists: (1902) and (1936) on the excess of good fits, (1986) on the distribution of chi-square values, (2004) on the 2:1 progeny tests, (2010) on repeated experiments, and the reassessment of the segregation ratios by et al. (2019). The collected papers in et al. (2008) give a common starting point.
  • Encode every count in and the letters to Nägeli as a single dataset, checking the German text and page images on against the manuscript held by the . Much depends on how one divides the reported totals into experimental units, since the chi-square results depend on whether counts are treated per plant, per experiment or pooled.
  • Reproduce the published models in one shared framework and state which assumptions each depends on. That alone would be a useful intermediate result: a public, rerunnable comparison of the main explanations on identical data.
  • Test the rival hypotheses against both anomalies at once: an assistant who knew the expected ratios, sequential stopping or repetition of deviant experiments, selective reporting, a different sampling model for the progeny tests, and botanical effects such as tetrad pollen, which (2007) tested experimentally and did not find. Without new documents the answer is likely to remain a set of procedures compatible with the data rather than a single verdict, and (2022) is a reminder that the framing of the question has its own history.
  • Search the records of the abbey, the Brno natural-history society, local schools and horticultural societies for mentions of assistants, seed exchanges and garden practice, and the papers of Nägeli and other correspondents for unpublished material, with Czech and German specialists reviewing any candidate finds.
  • Progress on the documentary side depends on the , the Augustinian Abbey and Czech and German archives making their holdings searchable and digitized, and on plant geneticists willing to run growing and counting trials that test the botanical explanations directly.

Existing work

  1. 1866
    Gregor Mendel, Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden, Verhandlungen des naturforschenden Vereines in Brünn 4: 3–47.The original paper reporting the pea-hybrid crosses whose counts Fisher later analysed.
  2. 1902
    W. F. R. Weldon, Mendel's Laws of Alternative Inheritance in Peas, Biometrika 1 (2): 228–233.First statistical scrutiny of Mendel's counts, noting their unusually close agreement with expectation and questioning the categories of pea characters.
  3. 1936
    R. A. Fisher, Has Mendel's Work Been Rediscovered?, Annals of Science 1 (2): 115–137.Found the aggregate fit too good to be chance and argued the 2:1 segregation ratios had been adjusted, suggesting an assistant may have known the expected results.
  4. 1986
    A. W. F. Edwards, Are Mendel's Results Really Too Close?, Biological Reviews 61 (4): 295–312.Re-examined the distribution of chi-square values across experiments and confirmed an excess of good fits.
  5. 2001
    Daniel J. Fairbanks and Bryce Rytting, Mendelian Controversies: A Botanical and Historical Review, American Journal of Botany 88 (5): 737–752.Reviewed the botanical feasibility of Mendel's experiments and argued that most charges of fraud are unfounded, while leaving the goodness-of-fit problem open.
  6. 2004
    Charles E. Novitski, On Fisher's Criticism of Mendel's Results with the Garden Pea, Genetics 166 (3): 1133–1136.Argued that Fisher's 2:1 versus 1.7:1 objection rests on assumptions about Mendel's procedure that need not hold.
  7. 2007
    Daniel J. Fairbanks and G. Bruce Schaalje, The Tetrad-Pollen Model Fails to Explain the Bias in Mendel's Pea (Pisum sativum) Experiments, Genetics 177 (4): 2531–2534.Experimental test showing that pollen sampling in self-pollinated peas is binomial, with no tetrad-pollen effect to explain the bias.
  8. 2008
    Allan Franklin, A. W. F. Edwards, Daniel J. Fairbanks, Daniel L. Hartl and Teddy Seidenfeld, Ending the Mendel–Fisher Controversy, Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press.Collected the key papers and concluded that Mendel did not fabricate data but that the too-good fit remains unexplained.
  9. 2010
    Ana M. Pires and João A. Branco, A Statistical Model to Explain the Mendel–Fisher Controversy, Statistical Science 25 (4).Proposed a model in which Mendel repeated experiments whose results deviated too far from expectation, reproducing the observed pattern.
  10. 2011
    T. H. Noel Ellis, Julie M. I. Hofer, Gail M. Timmerman-Vaughan, Clarice J. Coyne and Roger P. Hellens, Mendel, 150 Years On, Trends in Plant Science 16 (11): 590–596.Identified the molecular basis of most of Mendel's traits and discussed what this implies for scoring and counting.
  11. 2015
    Gregory Radick, Beyond the 'Mendel-Fisher Controversy', Science 350 (6257): 159–160.Argued that fixation on the statistics has obscured the history of how Mendelism was established and taught.
  12. 2016
    Peter J. van Dijk and T. H. Noel Ellis, The Full Breadth of Mendel's Genetics, Genetics 204 (4): 1327–1336.Reconstructed the scope of Mendel's experiments and argued that his aims were broader than the classic textbook account.
  13. 2018
    Peter J. van Dijk, Franz J. Weissing and T. H. Noel Ellis, How Mendel's Interest in Inheritance Grew out of Plant Improvement, Genetics 210 (2): 347–355.Located Mendel's experiments in Moravian plant-breeding culture, relevant to how and why he gathered and reported counts.
  14. 2019
    T. H. Noel Ellis, Julie M. I. Hofer, Eleni Vikeli, Michael J. Ambrose, Paola Higuera-Poveda, Luzie U. Wingen and Noam Chayut, Mendel's Pea Crosses: Varieties, Traits and Statistics, Hereditas 156: 33.Comprehensive re-examination of Mendel's segregation ratios, concluding that they do not differ remarkably from expectation.
  15. 2022
    Gregory Radick, Mendel the Fraud? A Social History of Truth in Genetics, Studies in History and Philosophy of Science 93: 39–46.Argues that the too-good-to-be-true debate became intense only from the 1960s, shaped by Cold War politics as much as by statistics.
