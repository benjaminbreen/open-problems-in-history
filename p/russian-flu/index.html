Did the age groups most at risk of dying change between waves of the “Russian flu”?

The pandemic that began in 1889 returned several times. City death registers could show whether each new wave took its heaviest toll on the same age groups.

Why it matters

In Madrid in the winter of 1889–90, people aged 70 and over had the largest rise in deaths from respiratory illness. In Denmark, the clearest surge came two winters later and fell most heavily on people aged 60 and over. Comparing successive waves in the same places could tell us whether the danger shifted from one generation to another as the pandemic wore on.

Why it is open

A city with more older residents can record more deaths among them even when the risk to each person is the same. Cities also used different age brackets and experienced outbreaks at different times. Doctors might record influenza, pneumonia or another cause for similar deaths.

What would count as a solution

For each wave from 1889 through 1894, estimate how many more people died than usual per 10,000 residents in each age group. Compare successive waves within each place, then see whether the same changes recur elsewhere. Report uncertainty, including places where the records are too thin to rank the age groups.

Potential approach

  • Use local medical reports to date each city’s waves. Start with and the published Swiss and Danish records. Pair the death counts with census populations in the same age brackets.
  • Use deaths from all causes and estimate the usual level for the same weeks in nearby pre-pandemic years. Check the pattern against respiratory deaths wherever both series survive.
  • Repeat the comparison with common age brackets, different baseline years and slightly different wave dates. Check local reports for other epidemics that might explain excess deaths in children or adults.
