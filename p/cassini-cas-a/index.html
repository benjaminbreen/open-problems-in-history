Did Cassini see the star that exploded in Cassiopeia in the 1670s?

Light from an exploding star reached Earth about 350 years ago. An astronomer’s notes could date the event and tell us how bright the star looked.

Science & knowledge · Europe · 1669–1683science

Could use help from astronomers and archivists and librarians.

Why it matters

The expanding cloud of debris is called Cassiopeia A. Astronomers can estimate the date of the explosion by tracing the debris back toward its centre. A dated observation of the star itself would give them an independent clock.

Why it is open

In June 1671, Jean Gallois reported that the astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini had seen three stars missing from earlier counts of Cassiopeia. His description leaves the brightest one’s position uncertain. survives, but his regular Paris journal begins in September 1671. A first reading found four October and November entries about Cassiopeia, including a night lost to clouds. None identifies an exploding star.

What would count as a solution

A dated entry would need enough neighbouring stars or measurements to place the object at the remnant and distinguish it from known stars. A search that comes up empty can still establish which nights, fields and brightnesses the journals cover.

Potential approach

  • Read alongside the eight observing journals and the June report. Record each observing date, comparison star and interruption for weather.
  • Reconstruct the sky on each date and test the positional error on stars Cassini named.
  • Publish the matching passages and the gaps in coverage, especially the months before Gallois went to press.
