How much iron did Song China produce in the eleventh century?

Robert Hartwell's estimate of about 125,000 tons of iron in 1078 is widely repeated, but it rests on fiscal records whose relation to total output is disputed.

Economy · East Asia · 960–1127science

Why it matters

Hartwell's figure is the main quantitative support for the idea of a Northern Song 'industrial revolution' and for claims that Chinese iron output in 1078 rivalled that of Britain in the late eighteenth century. It is used in world histories, in comparative arguments about the Great Divergence, and in environmental histories of deforestation and the shift from charcoal to coal. If output was much lower, or largely charcoal-fuelled, the narrative of a Song coal-and-iron breakthrough that later stalled needs revision.

Why it is open

The figure is extrapolated from iron receipts recorded for state monopoly and tax purposes in compilations such as the and Wenxian tongkao, which record what the state collected, not what was produced, and Hartwell's multiplier from receipts to output is an assumption. Donald Wagner argued that these administrative figures cannot be scaled up so simply, and Tim 's review of four decades of citation found that the estimate has been repeated far more often than tested. Archaeological evidence for Song ironworks is patchy: few smelting sites have been excavated and published in detail, and slag volumes, furnace capacities and fuel types have not been surveyed systematically.

What would count as a solution

An output estimate for the 1070s, with explicit error bounds, built from at least two independent lines of evidence (the fiscal record re-read prefecture by prefecture with a stated model of collection rates, and archaeological slag-heap volumes and furnace capacities from surveyed sites), with the two lines agreeing within their stated uncertainties.

Potential approach

  • Any revision begins with the case (1962) and (1966) built from receipt lists in the and Wenxian tongkao, and his longer cycle in (1967). The critiques in (2001) and (2008), the reception history traced by (2007) and the survey of mining by (1999) set out where that reasoning is weakest.
  • Extract every mention of ironworks (ye, jian, wu), quotas and receipts from the Song huiyao jigao, Xu zizhi tongjian changbian, Wenxian tongkao and Song gazetteers, through and the , and tie each to a prefecture and date using (2016). The decision that shapes the data is how each figure is classed: quota, actual receipt, tax share of private output, or narrative report.
  • Several models of what the receipts mean then compete. Hartwell's multiplier treats taxed iron as a fixed fraction of private output; a lower reading treats receipts as close to the output of state works, with private production largely unrecorded; a third allows the fraction to vary with collection rules. Figures that shift with changes in administration rather than with regional demand would count against a fixed multiplier.
  • A prefecture-by-prefecture register of attested Song iron sites, linking documentary references to published site reports, would be useful even if total output stays uncertain, because it shows where production was concentrated and where fieldwork would test the most.
  • Archaeology gives a second line of evidence. Slag volumes and furnace capacities at districts around near Handan, in Cizhou, Shanxi and Henan, and at charcoal-fuelled bloomery sites such as in Fujian, together with fuel tracers of the kind used by (2003) and (2019), could bound output and the share smelted with coal. The answer will remain an order-of-magnitude range, but one with stated error bounds.
  • That second line depends on archaeometallurgists, such as those at the University of Science and Technology Beijing, and on provincial cultural-relics bureaus permitting survey, sampling and radiocarbon dating. Historians of Song fiscal administration need to review how the textual figures are classed.

Existing work

  1. 1962
    Robert Hartwell, A Revolution in the Chinese Iron and Coal Industries during the Northern Sung, 960–1126 A.D., Journal of Asian Studies 21 (2): 153–162.Founding statement of the Northern Song iron and coal 'revolution', based on fiscal and descriptive sources.
  2. 1966
    Robert Hartwell, Markets, Technology, and the Structure of Enterprise in the Development of the Eleventh-Century Chinese Iron and Steel Industry, Journal of Economic History 26 (1): 29–58.Developed the output estimate for 1078 and compared eleventh-century Chinese expansion favourably with early modern England.
  3. 1967
    Robert Hartwell, A Cycle of Economic Change in Imperial China: Coal and Iron in Northeast China, 750–1350, Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient 10: 102–159.Argued that coal and iron production in the northeast peaked in the Northern Song and then declined, at least per capita.
  4. 1999
    Peter J. Golas, Science and Civilisation in China, vol. 5, part 13: Mining, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Comprehensive history of Chinese mining, including Song coal and iron ore extraction, from written and archaeological sources.
  5. 2001
    Donald B. Wagner, The Administration of the Iron Industry in Eleventh-Century China, Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient 44 (2): 175–197.Re-examined the Song administrative sources on which Hartwell relied and questioned how far the recorded figures can be converted into total production.
  6. 2003
    Michael L. Wayman and Helen Wang, Cast iron coins of Song dynasty China: a metallurgical study, Historical Metallurgy 37 (1): 6–24.Analysed 37 Song iron coins and inferred from sulphur content that Shaanxi mints used coal- or coke-smelted iron while southeastern mints used charcoal-smelted iron.
  7. 2003
    Donald B. Wagner, Chinese blast furnaces from the 10th to the 14th century, Historical Metallurgy 37 (1): 25–37.Reviewed excavated and standing Song–Yuan furnaces, including the Kuangshancun furnace in Hebei, and the shift toward mineral fuel and water-powered bellows.
  8. 2007
    Tim Wright, An Economic Cycle in Imperial China? Revisiting Robert Hartwell on Iron and Coal, Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient 50: 398–423.Traced how Hartwell's estimate was received and reused over forty years and called for a consistent long-run series of coal and iron estimates.
  9. 2008
    Donald B. Wagner, Science and Civilisation in China, vol. 5, part 11: Ferrous Metallurgy, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Standard history of Chinese iron and steel in political and economic context, with extended treatment of the Song industry and its sources.
  10. 2016
    CHGIS (Fairbank Center for Chinese Studies, Harvard University, and Center for Historical Geographical Studies, Fudan University), China Historical GIS, Version 6, Harvard Dataverse.Time-series dataset of administrative units, including prefectures and counties across dynasties, for placing Song iron sites and quotas on a map; free for academic use.
  11. 2019
    Siran Liu, Thilo Rehren, Dashu Qin, Jianli Chen et al., Coal-fuelled crucible lead-silver smelting in 12th–13th century China: A technological innovation in the age of deforestation, Journal of Archaeological Science 104: 75–84.Excavated evidence from Hebei for coal-fuelled non-ferrous smelting, while noting that most iron smelters of the period still used charcoal-fired furnaces.
