How common was sleeping in two shifts before industrialization?

People once wrote of waking from a “first sleep.” Their accounts could tell us when sleeping through the night became an expectation and whose nights followed a different pattern.

Science & knowledge · Europe · 1300–1900

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers and statisticians and data scientists.

Why it matters

Historian A. Roger Ekirch argues that many Europeans slept twice at night, with a waking interval between. Niall asks whether some of the same passages describe an occasional interruption or simply the deepest part of sleep. Their disagreement concerns how common the habit was, and among whom.

Why it is open

An initial search of transcribed early English books returned 372 “first sleep” passages in 307 books. A phrase count includes translations, reprints and figures of speech. The strongest evidence would be a writer who describes waking, doing something and going back to bed, and tells us how often this happened.

What would count as a solution

For defined periods, regions and kinds of writing, measure how often independent accounts describe two sleeps and a waking interval. Compare these with accounts of continuous nights and accidental waking. Diaries with many entries can say more about regularity than a single use of a phrase.

Potential approach

  • Begin with English printed books and distinguish separate witnesses from reprints. Have readers independently classify each passage before comparing it with Ekirch’s examples.
  • Check diaries, household accounts and court testimony for nights described without the words “first sleep.” Record season, occupation and artificial lighting where the writer mentions them.
  • Test the same distinctions in other European languages before extending an English finding to Europe as a whole.
