How often did “simultaneous” inventions follow an earlier design in a foreign journal?

Two inventors sometimes patented similar machines months apart. A drawing published between their filings could change who gets credit and show how news of an invention travelled.

Science & knowledge · Global · 1830–1900

Could use help from economists and archivists and librarians.

Why it matters

Mechanics and engineers read illustrated journals that translated and reprinted foreign designs. A pair of patents filed close together might represent independent work, one inventor filing abroad, or someone adapting a machine they had seen in print. The proportions matter to histories of how new technologies spread.

Why it is open

Filing, publication and local availability have different dates. Two people trying to solve the same mechanical problem might use familiar parts in similar ways. A translated description may hide a debt, while identical engravings may be openly credited.

What would count as a solution

For a defined sample of priority disputes, estimate how many later designs demonstrably drew on a foreign publication. Report separately the cases with an earlier parallel, a plausible opportunity to read it and evidence of actual borrowing. Give the uncertain cases a place in the count.

Potential approach

  • Choose a group of machines and a sampling rule before looking for matches. Link filings by the same inventor or agent across the US, Britain, France and Germany.
  • Compare patent drawings and claims with dated issues of Dingler’s Polytechnisches Journal, Mechanics’ Magazine and Scientific American. Check actual issue dates and circulation.
  • Look for a shared unusual error, a letter mentioning the article or an acknowledged adaptation. Check whether the later inventor had already built a prototype before the article appeared. Compare the match rate with unrelated designs in the same trade.
