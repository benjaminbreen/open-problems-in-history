What do the thousands of unread Shang oracle-bone graphs mean?

Of roughly 4,000 to 4,500 distinct graphs in the Shang divination inscriptions, only about 1,100 to 1,700 are generally accepted as read; most of the rest are rare and unread.

Languages & scripts · East Asia · 1250–1046 BCEdeciphermentdigitization

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers, computational linguists and archivists and librarians.

Why it matters

The oracle-bone inscriptions from Anyang, written between about 1250 and 1046 BCE, are the earliest substantial body of Chinese writing and the main contemporary source for Shang kingship, ancestor cult, warfare, agriculture, kinship, calendar and geography. Most unread graphs are rare, but many are personal names, place names, sacrifice names and verbs, so each secure reading can fix a lineage, a site or a ritual practice that is otherwise known only from later texts. The readings also bear on how the Chinese script was formed and on the relation of Shang writing to the bronze inscriptions and Warring States manuscripts.

Why it is open

Counts differ with the criteria used: Jiang Yubin gives about 4,000 graphs with about 1,160 widely accepted, Yuan about 4,400 with nearly 1,700 read, and computer-science papers about 4,500 with 1,500 to 1,600 read. The unread graphs are mostly hapax or rare forms, many of them proper names, so there is little context to test a reading and often no descendant form in later script. The corpus is scattered among about 70,000 published pieces held in many institutions, much of it fragmentary and published under different numbering systems, and identification has long rested on individual philologists' arguments rather than on stated criteria. Since 2016 the National Museum of Chinese Writing at Anyang has offered 100,000 yuan for each accepted new decipherment, but only a handful of awards had been reported by the end of 2023.

What would count as a solution

A set of readings for previously unread graphs, each fixed in writing before testing, that (a) passes the Anyang prize panel's expert review or equivalent peer review, (b) predicts how the graph is used in inscriptions withheld from the analysis (its grammatical slot, collocations and parallels in matching charges), and (c) is consistent with a traceable form in bronze or Warring States script. Readings of proper names that cannot meet (b) or (c) should be reported as such rather than counted.

Potential approach

  • The corpus is largely fixed in print and increasingly online. Guo (1978) and the facsimile corpus of Huang (2022), with 70,659 pieces sorted by script group, supply the texts; Yu (1996) gathers earlier interpretations graph by graph, and (2014) lists the graph forms. A first task is a single concordance linking these to the database at Anyang Normal University and to museum catalogue numbers.
  • Context comparison should follow script-group chronology. Huang (1991) and the typology built on it let each attestation be dated by diviner group, and the pairing of positive and negative charges described by (1978) and (2010) gives natural parallels in which an unread graph sits in the same slot as a read one.
  • (2000) set out criteria for a rigorous decipherment, and Qiu (1988) the method of tracing a graph through later forms. Teams can apply these systematically, aligning each unread graph with candidate descendants in the bronze and Warring States corpora and recording where the chain breaks. The edited by (2019) are a well-documented test bed, since they were excavated as a group and fully published.
  • AI work so far is strongest at the visual layer. The HUST-OBC dataset of et al. (2024), the diffusion model of et al. (2024) and the multi-stage AlphaOracle system of Liu et al. (2026) propose candidate modern equivalents from form; agents could combine these with corpus-wide context search, but every proposed reading should be registered before it is scored on withheld inscriptions.
  • (2011) showed that practice inscriptions and scribal habits affect graph forms, so forms that look new may be variants. Results should go to oracle-bone specialists and, where they claim a new decipherment, to the Anyang prize panel, whose first two rounds (2022 and 2023) give a benchmark for what counts as accepted.

Who can help

philologists and epigraphers
For each candidate graph, compile every attestation by diviner group with its bronze and Warring States descendants, and fix proposed readings in writing before they are tested.
computational linguists
Build held-out tests in which a proposed reading must predict an unread graph's slot, collocations and parallel charges in inscriptions withheld from the analysis.
archivists and librarians
Link rubbings, photographs and catalogue numbers for the same piece across the Heji, the Mobendaxi, Yinqi Wenyuan and museum databases into one open concordance.

  1. 1978
    David N. Keightley, Sources of Shang History: The Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of Bronze Age China, Berkeley: University of California Press.The standard English guide to the bones as artefacts and texts: preparation, cracking, charge forms, dating and the use of the inscriptions as historical evidence.
  2. 1978
    Guo Moruo (ed.), Hu Houxuan (chief editor), Jiaguwen heji 甲骨文合集, 13 vols. Beijing: Zhonghua shuju, 1978–1982.Collected rubbings and facsimiles of the then-known corpus arranged by period and subject; its numbering ('Heji') is still the standard reference for most inscriptions.
  3. 1988
    Qiu Xigui, Wenzixue gaiyao 文字學概要, Beijing: Shangwu yinshuguan; English trans. by Gilbert L. Mattos and Jerry Norman as Chinese Writing (Society for the Study of Early China, 2000).Systematic account of how Chinese graphs are formed and change, the methodological basis for tracing oracle-bone forms into later scripts.
  4. 1991
    Huang Tianshu, Yinxu wang buci de fenlei yu duandai 殷墟王卜辭的分類與斷代, Taipei: Wenjin chubanshe.Classified the royal inscriptions by script group before dating them, the typology now used to place each attestation of a graph in time.
  5. 1996
    Yu Xingwu (ed.), with commentary by Yao Xiaosui, Jiagu wenzi gulin 甲骨文字詁林, 4 vols. Beijing: Zhonghua shuju.Compendium of published interpretations of each oracle-bone graph, with editorial judgements; the starting point for knowing what has already been proposed.
  6. 2000
    Ken-ichi Takashima, Towards a More Rigorous Methodology of Deciphering Oracle-Bone Inscriptions, T'oung Pao 86 (4): 363–399.Argued that decipherment must combine graphic, phonological and syntactic evidence and set out criteria for testing a proposed reading.
  7. 2010
    Ken-ichi Takashima and Paul L-M. Serruys, Studies of Fascicle Three of Inscriptions from the Yin Ruins, 2 vols. Taipei: Institute of History and Philology, Academia Sinica.Full translation and grammatical commentary on the Bingbian plastra from pit YH127, a model of close reading of complete charge sets.

    Adam Smith, The Evidence for Scribal Training at Anyang, in Li Feng and David Prager Branner (eds.), Writing and Literacy in Early China. Seattle: University of Washington Press, 181–213.Identified practice inscriptions and copying exercises, showing how scribal training shaped graph forms and variants.
  9. 2014
    Liu Zhao (chief ed.), Xin jiaguwen bian 新甲骨文編 (revised edition), Fuzhou: Fujian renmin chubanshe (first ed. 2009).Graph list giving the attested forms of each recognized graph with their sources; widely used as an inventory of read graphs.
