How many Indigenous people were enslaved in the Americas between 1492 and 1900?

The best current estimate, 2.5 to 5 million enslaved Indigenous people, is the only hemispheric figure, and it rests on regional extrapolation rather than a count.

Demography · Americas · 1492–1900digitization

Could use help from archivists and librarians and demographers.

Why it matters

The scale of Indigenous enslavement determines how far the demographic collapse of the Americas should be attributed to coerced labor, displacement and violence rather than to epidemic disease alone. It affects the comparison between Indigenous and African slavery in the colonial economies of the Caribbean, Brazil, Chile, northern Mexico, the American Southwest and the English colonies of the Southeast and New England. It also bears on how historians read Spanish and Portuguese abolitions of Indian slavery (1542, 1570, 1755) that were repeatedly evaded through categories such as just war, rescate, encomienda, peonage and 'adoption'.

Why it is open

Indigenous slavery was frequently illegal or ambiguously legal, so it was recorded under euphemisms (criados, piezas, naborías, administrados, captives) or not recorded at all, and it was dispersed across many small transactions rather than concentrated in shipping. The surviving evidence is scattered among notarial protocols, lawsuits, baptismal registers, probate inventories, military reports and Indigenous testimony in Spanish, Portuguese, French, English and Indigenous languages, mostly undigitized or uncatalogued at item level. 's 2.5–5 million figure, which he presents as a cautious range, therefore aggregates regional estimates of very different quality, and several regions (Amazonia, the Río de la Plata, Central America after 1550) have no systematic count.

What would count as a solution

A published, source-linked database of individual enslaved Indigenous people and enslaving events, organized by region and period, from which documented minimums can be computed and combined with explicit, testable multipliers for under-recording, producing a hemispheric range that others can recompute region by region.

Potential approach

  • Start from the regional reconstructions historians have already built, among them (1971) on Mapuche captives in Chile, (1988) on the Paulista captive economy, (2002) on the English Southeast, 's (1990) name-by-name register of enslaved people in French Canada, roughly two-thirds of them Indigenous, and (2021) on the early Spanish Caribbean. Record for each the sources, method and coverage behind the figures that (2016) aggregates, so that the regions with no systematic count are explicit.
  • Build with regional specialists a glossary of the terms under which bondage was recorded (indio esclavo, pieza, naboría, criado, administrado, panis, gentio da terra), dated and placed, since many of these terms also described free or semi-free people and the same word could mean different statuses in different places.
  • Pilot on series that are partly digitized and already well studied, such as the and records for the period of legal Mapuche slavery (1608–1683), on which (2017) and his collaborators have worked, or the . Agents can transcribe, flag candidate entries and link the same person across sales, baptisms, lawsuits and inventories, with historians reviewing each link and checking totals against published regional counts.
  • Use the overlap between record types in these pilots to estimate how many enslaved people a given series misses. That correction is what turns a list of documented individuals into a population estimate.
  • Model the hemispheric total as a range built up region by region, each with stated assumptions about record survival and under-registration, and publish the linked records and code so that other teams can revise any region.
  • Extending this beyond the pilots depends on archives digitizing notarial and judicial series in , , Lima, and , and on working with Indigenous communities, and with projects such as that are already assembling records of enslaved people, on how those records are described and used.

Existing work

  1. 1913
    Almon Wheeler Lauber, Indian Slavery in Colonial Times within the Present Limits of the United States, New York: Columbia University (Studies in History, Economics and Public Law).First survey of Indigenous enslavement in the English, French and Spanish colonies north of Mexico.
  2. 1955
    W. L. McDowell (ed.), Journals of the Commissioners of the Indian Trade, September 20, 1710-August 29, 1718, Columbia: South Carolina Archives Department (Colonial Records of South Carolina).Printed edition of the Commissioners' journals, which record purchases of and trade in Indigenous captives in Carolina.
  3. 1971
    William L. Sherman, Indian Slavery and the Cerrato Reforms, Hispanic American Historical Review 51 (1): 25–50.Showed the scale of Indian slavery in Guatemala and the effects of the New Laws' enforcement in the 1540s.
  4. 1971
    Álvaro Jara, Guerra y sociedad en Chile: la transformación de la Guerra de Arauco y la esclavitud de los indios, Santiago: Editorial Universitaria (first published Paris, 1961).The foundational study of the legal enslavement of Mapuche prisoners of war after the 1608 decree.
  5. 1978
    Stuart B. Schwartz, Indian Labor and New World Plantations: European Demands and Indian Responses in Northeastern Brazil, American Historical Review 83 (1).Documented the reliance of early Bahian sugar plantations on enslaved and coerced Indigenous labor before the transition to African slavery.
  6. 1979
    William L. Sherman, Forced Native Labor in Sixteenth-Century Central America, Lincoln: University of Nebraska Press.Regional study including the export of enslaved Indigenous people from Nicaragua and Honduras to Panama and Peru.
  7. 1988
    John M. Monteiro, From Indian to Slave: Forced Native Labour and Colonial Society in São Paulo during the Seventeenth Century, Slavery & Abolition 9 (2): 105–127.Used São Paulo probate inventories to show the scale of bandeirante enslavement; expanded in Negros da terra (1994).
  8. 1990
    Marcel Trudel, Dictionnaire des esclaves et de leurs propriétaires au Canada français, Montreal: Hurtubise HMH.A name-by-name register of enslaved people and their owners in French Canada, most of them Indigenous panis; reviewers have questioned some of its identifications.
  9. 2002
    Alan Gallay, The Indian Slave Trade: The Rise of the English Empire in the American South, 1670–1717, New Haven: Yale University Press.Argued that between roughly 24,000 and 51,000 Indigenous people were captured and sold through the Carolina trade between 1670 and 1715, many of them exported to the Caribbean.
  10. 2002
    James F. Brooks, Captives and Cousins: Slavery, Kinship, and Community in the Southwest Borderlands, Chapel Hill: Omohundro Institute / University of North Carolina Press.Used New Mexico baptismal registers and legal records to trace captive exchange among Spanish, Pueblo, Navajo, Apache and Comanche communities into the nineteenth century.
  11. 2011
    Neil L. Whitehead, Indigenous Slavery in South America, 1492–1820, in David Eltis and Stanley L. Engerman (eds.), The Cambridge World History of Slavery, vol. 3 (Cambridge University Press), 248–272.Continental synthesis covering both Indigenous and European-driven slaving in South America.
  12. 2012
    Brett Rushforth, Bonds of Alliance: Indigenous and Atlantic Slaveries in New France, Chapel Hill: Omohundro Institute / University of North Carolina Press.Reconstructed thousands of enslaved Indigenous people (panis) in French colonial records from the Great Lakes to the Caribbean.
  13. 2015
    Nancy E. van Deusen, Global Indios: The Indigenous Struggle for Justice in Sixteenth-Century Spain, Durham: Duke University Press.Analyzes more than one hundred freedom suits brought in Castile between 1530 and 1585 by enslaved Indigenous people transported to Iberia.
  14. 2015
    Margaret Ellen Newell, Brethren by Nature: New England Indians, Colonists, and the Origins of American Slavery, Ithaca: Cornell University Press.Shows the centrality of Indigenous enslavement and export in New England, especially after the Pequot War and King Philip's War.
  15. 2016
    Andrés Reséndez, The Other Slavery: The Uncovered Story of Indian Enslavement in America, Boston: Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.Hemispheric synthesis proposing that between 2.5 and 5 million Indigenous people were enslaved from 1492 to 1900, excluding encomienda and repartimiento labor.
  16. 2017
    Arne Bialuschewski and Linford D. Fisher, Guest Editors' Introduction: New Directions in the History of Native American Slavery Studies, Ethnohistory 64 (1): 1–17.Introduces a special issue on seventeenth-century Indigenous slavery and surveys the hemispheric literature in several languages.
  17. 2017
    Jaime Valenzuela Márquez (ed.), América en diásporas: esclavitudes y migraciones forzadas en Chile y otras regiones americanas (siglos XVI–XIX), Santiago: RIL editores and Instituto de Historia, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile.Collected studies of Indigenous and African slavery and forced migration, including Valenzuela's work on enslaved Mapuche women before the Real Audiencia of Chile.
  18. 2021
    Erin Woodruff Stone, Captives of Conquest: Slavery in the Early Modern Spanish Caribbean, Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press.Argues that upwards of 250,000 Indigenous people were enslaved in the Spanish Caribbean between the 1490s and the New Laws of 1542.
