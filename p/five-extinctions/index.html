What new evidence can fix when and why five historically extinct species disappeared?

The dates, ranges and causes of extinction of the dodo, great auk, Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon and Carolina parakeet still rest on thin, contested written records.

Chronology · Global · 1500–1945science

Could use help from archivists and librarians, geneticists, statisticians and data scientists, philologists and epigraphers, archaeologists, computational linguists and GIS specialists.

Why it matters

These five species carry much of the historical argument about human-caused extinction. The dodo was central to the recognition that species could disappear at all, from (1848) onward; the great auk inquiry of Wolley and in 1858 shaped the first bird-protection campaigns in Britain; Steller's sea cow is the standard case of a marine mammal exterminated within decades of scientific description; and the passenger pigeon and Carolina parakeet anchor accounts of market hunting, habitat loss and the origins of wildlife law in the United States. Their dates and causes are reused as calibration points for statistical methods that estimate extinction from sightings, and as evidence in debates over whether hunting alone, ecological cascades or earlier natural decline did the damage.

Why it is open

Each standing date depends on a few records whose identification or provenance is questionable. The dodo's last accepted sighting is either Volkert Evertsz's 1662 encounter on an islet off Mauritius or undescribed 'dodaersen' in the hunting lists of the Dutch commander Isaac Lamotius in 1685–88, which (2020) argue were red hens. The 1768 date for the sea cow derives from a later report printed by Martin Sauer, and nineteenth-century claims of survivors were contested by (1887). Great auk sightings after the Eldey killing of June 1844, passenger pigeon reports after the last confirmed wild bird of 1900, and Carolina parakeet reports from the 1920s and 1930s have never been graded consistently. The relevant sources are scattered across Dutch, Danish, Icelandic, Russian and American archives in five languages, and much of the post-1850 record sits in newspapers and sporting periodicals that were not searchable until recently.

What would count as a solution

For any one of the five species, a solution is new evidence that a panel of historians and biologists judges to change significantly what is known about its date, range or cause of extinction. The panel's tests are concrete: a securely dated and identified record later than the currently accepted last record; a demonstration that a standing last record is misdated, misattributed or refers to another animal; a documented provenance (collector, date, locality) for an undated or misattributed specimen, ideally confirmed by ancient DNA; or a quantitative reconstruction of the decline, built from graded and published records, that discriminates between rival causes (for example hunting rates against habitat loss, disease or prior natural decline).

Potential approach

  • The human literature already supplies baselines for each species: (2003), , Martill and Dewdney (2004) and (2020) on the dodo; (1861), et al. (2019) and (2024) on the great auk; (1887), (2006) and , Burdin and Doak (2016) on the sea cow; (1955) and et al. (2017) on the passenger pigeon; (2004) and et al. (2021) on the Carolina parakeet.
  • Research agents can mine the digitized record at scale: from Mauritius in the Nationaal Archief through ; Icelandic and Danish administrative and trade records and in the Alfred Newton Papers for the great auk; and voyage records for the Commander Islands; and , game-market reports and sporting periodicals in the for the pigeon and parakeet.
  • Every candidate record becomes an entry in a graded sighting database (specimen, dated eyewitness description, undescribed report, hearsay) with its source, language and identification argument, published openly so that others can regrade it.
  • Sighting-record estimators, applied to North American birds by , Roberts and Reed (2010), should be tested first on species whose last individuals are documented, such as the captive passenger pigeon and parakeet, before being applied to the dodo, great auk and sea cow, and results reported across grading schemes rather than for one preferred dataset.
  • Confirmation of specimen provenance and of pre-contact range needs geneticists and museum curators: ancient DNA and radiocarbon dating of skins, eggs and midden bones, matched against sale catalogues, accession registers and collectors' correspondence, following the method used to identify the Eldey birds.

Who can help

archivists and librarians
Inventory the VOC Mauritius correspondence, Icelandic district records and Russian voyage papers that mention these species, and report which series remain untranscribed or uncatalogued.
geneticists
Sequence dated museum skins, eggs and midden bones of the five species to test specimen provenance and when populations began to decline, extending the Eldey-pair identifications.
statisticians and data scientists
Benchmark sighting-record extinction estimators on graded databases that mix certain and doubtful records, using species with documented last individuals as controls.
philologists and epigraphers
Decide from Dutch, Danish, Icelandic and eighteenth-century Russian usage which animal each name in the late records denotes, starting with the 1680s Mauritian 'dodaersen'.
archaeologists
Date great auk and sea cow bones from North Atlantic and North Pacific middens to establish ranges before European contact and when local populations disappeared.
computational linguists
Build name-variant searches and HTR models that find these species in Chronicling America, VOC transcriptions and Icelandic periodicals, and report recall against hand-checked samples.
GIS specialists
Georeference every graded sighting and specimen of the passenger pigeon and Carolina parakeet and map range contraction by decade against land clearance and rail lines.

    H. E. Strickland and A. G. Melville, The Dodo and Its Kindred, London: Reeve, Benham and Reeve.Gathered the travellers' accounts, images and remains of the dodo and solitaire and established them as real birds made extinct by human activity.
  2. 1861
    Alfred Newton, Abstract of Mr. J. Wolley's Researches in Iceland respecting the Gare-fowl or Great Auk, Ibis 3 (4): 374–399.Published the 1858 interviews with the men who took the last pair on Eldey in 1844, the source of the standard last record.
  3. 1887
    Leonhard Stejneger, How the Great Northern Sea-Cow (Rytina) Became Exterminated, The American Naturalist 21 (12): 1047–1054.Argued from fieldwork on Bering Island and Russian sources that the sea cow was hunted out by about 1768, rejecting claims of later survival.
  4. 1955
    A. W. Schorger, The Passenger Pigeon: Its Natural History and Extinction, Madison: University of Wisconsin Press.Compiled newspaper, diary and market records on nesting, netting and shipments, still the main documentary base for the pigeon's decline.
  5. 2003
    David L. Roberts and Andrew R. Solow, When did the dodo become extinct?, Nature 426: 245.Applied optimal linear estimation to dated sightings and put the dodo's extinction at about 1690, some thirty years after the 1662 sighting.
  6. 2004
    Julian P. Hume, David M. Martill and Christopher Dewdney, Dutch diaries and the demise of the dodo, Nature 429.Used the hunting records kept by Isaac Lamotius to argue that dodos were still being taken on Mauritius until 1688.
  7. 2004
    Noel F. R. Snyder, The Carolina Parakeet: Glimpses of a Vanished Bird, Princeton: Princeton University Press.Assessed the historical record of the parakeet's range and decline, including disputed late reports from Florida and South Carolina, and weighed hunting, habitat loss and disease as causes.
  8. 2006
    Samuel T. Turvey and Claire L. Risley, Modelling the extinction of Steller's sea cow, Biology Letters 2 (1): 94–97.Modelled eighteenth-century Russian hunting records and concluded that overhunting at more than seven times the sustainable rate sufficed to cause extinction by 1768.
  9. 2010
    Chris S. Elphick, David L. Roberts and J. Michael Reed, Estimated dates of recent extinctions for North American and Hawaiian birds, Biological Conservation 143 (3): 617–624.Compiled sighting records for 52 rare North American and Hawaiian bird taxa and estimated extinction dates for the 38 with adequate data, treating uncertain records explicitly.

    James A. Estes, Alexander Burdin and Daniel F. Doak, Sea otters, kelp forests, and the extinction of Steller's sea cow, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 113 (4): 880–885.Argued that the removal of sea otters, by triggering urchin-driven kelp loss, could have contributed to the sea cow's decline, against a purely hunting-based account.
