What is the absolute chronology of early second-millennium BCE Mesopotamia?

Competing High, Middle, Low and Ultra-Low chronologies place Hammurabi and the fall of Babylon up to about 150 years apart; no single model integrates all the evidence.

Chronology · Middle East · 2000–1500 BCEscience

Could use help from astronomers and archaeologists.

Why it matters

The relative sequence of Old Assyrian, Old Babylonian and Kassite rulers is well established, but its anchor in absolute years is not, so every date before about 1400 BCE in Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Syria and the Levant inherits the uncertainty. Synchronisms with Egypt, the Hittite sack of Babylon, the dating of the Santorini eruption debate, climate-and-collapse arguments and comparisons with radiocarbon-dated archaeology all depend on which option is chosen. The four usual options put the fall of Babylon at 1651, 1595, 1531 or 1499 BCE.

Why it is open

The are corrupt and allow several solutions roughly 56 or 64 years apart, and month-length and eclipse arguments depend on contested readings and observational assumptions. Assyrian King List distance figures contain uncertain reign lengths, and the Kültepe eponym lists must be joined to Old Babylonian sources through a small number of synchronisms. Dendrochronological sequences from Anatolian sites were long only floating, and their wiggle-matched radiocarbon dates carry their own calibration and regional-offset uncertainties.

What would count as a solution

A published, reproducible Bayesian model that ingests the Venus data, lunar month lengths, eclipse candidates, king-list and eponym-list distance numbers, dendrochronological sequences and radiocarbon dates with explicit priors, returns a posterior for a fixed anchor (such as Ammisaduqa year 1) with a stated uncertainty under 10 years, and remains stable when individual lines of evidence are removed.

Potential approach

  • The textual framework is already well mapped: (2009) sets out the king lists, eponym lists, year names and synchronisms, , Hertel and Larsen (2012) rebuilt the Old Assyrian eponym sequence with the help of KEL G from (2008), and (2010) re-edited every copy of the . Teams can build directly on these, and on the dated texts in and the .
  • Compile one machine-readable dataset of every chronologically relevant datum: each Venus copy and its variant readings, month-length records from Old Babylonian archives, eponym-list entries, reign lengths and cross-dated synchronisms, each with full citation and a note on how secure the reading is. Deciding how to score a damaged or disputed reading will shape the result more than any later statistics.
  • The rival solutions, the High chronology of (1928) and (1982), the Middle and Low-Middle options argued by Barjamovic, Hertel and Larsen (2012) and (2013), and the Ultra-Low chronology of et al. (1998), each make predictions about Venus visibility, lunar month lengths and the spacing of Assyrian eponyms. Testing all of them against the same dataset, as (2000) and (2000) did against parts of it, would show where they genuinely diverge.
  • Encode the textual constraints alongside the dendro-radiocarbon wiggle-matches of et al. (2016) in a Bayesian framework such as OxCal, described by (2009), calibrated with IntCal20 from et al. (2020). Modelling the regional offsets identified by et al. (2020) explicitly matters, since a few decades of offset is the size of the gap between neighbouring chronologies.
  • Sensitivity analysis is a useful result in its own right: it would show which tablets, readings and samples actually drive the answer, and which long-debated items make no difference. The answer will most likely remain a narrowed range of candidate dates rather than a single year, and Assyriologists and radiocarbon specialists should review both the coding and the model.
  • Settling the remaining range requires new radiocarbon and dendrochronological measurements on securely contexted short-lived samples and timbers from Kültepe, Acemhöyük and Babylonian sites, which depends on excavation teams, the and radiocarbon laboratories, and on museums allowing collation of the tablets themselves.

Who can help

astronomers
Test the High, Middle, Low-Middle and Ultra-Low chronologies against every copy of the Venus observations, the Old Babylonian month lengths and the eclipse candidates in one dataset.
archaeologists
Combine the Anatolian dendro-radiocarbon wiggle-matches with the textual constraints in a Bayesian model, stating calibration and regional-offset assumptions and checking stability when each line of evidence is removed.
