How many people died in the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961?

Estimates of excess deaths in China's Great Leap famine range from under 20 million to 45 million, depending on sources, baselines and assumptions about registration.

Demography · East Asia · 1958–1962

Why it matters

The death toll is the largest single quantity in the history of the People's Republic and anchors comparative work on twentieth-century famines, state violence and the demography of socialist planning. Economic studies of the famine's causes (procurement, grain exports, communal dining, local political incentives) use the geographic pattern of excess mortality as their dependent variable, so their conclusions inherit any error in that pattern. Long-run studies of fetal and childhood famine exposure also depend on county-level measures of severity.

Why it is open

The People's Republic has never published an official death count, and the main sources were produced under the same political pressures that caused the famine. Reconstructions from the 1953, 1964 and 1982 censuses and the 1982 One-per-Thousand Fertility Survey gave totals of roughly 23–30 million (Coale, Ashton et al., Banister, Peng Xizhe); later work using county gazetteers and provincial archives produced 32.5 million (Cao Shuji), 36 million (Yang Jisheng) and at least 45 million for 1958–62 (Dikötter), while other authors argue that household-registration catch-up after 1958 inflates recorded deaths and that the true figure is much lower. Estimates differ less over the evidence than over the counterfactual baseline mortality, the treatment of births foregone, migration, and how to correct for deaths registered late.

What would count as a solution

A published county-level dataset of registered population, births and deaths for 1954–1965 covering all counties with surviving data, with excess-mortality estimates computed under a single stated set of assumptions about baseline mortality and registration error, whose national total reconciles with the 1953, 1964 and 1982 census age structures within stated uncertainty bounds.

Potential approach

  • Agents can extract annual population, birth and death figures from the post-1980 county gazetteers and provincial statistical compilations into a common table, recording page references and noting where gazetteers revise or contradict one another.
  • Historians can then fix one set of baseline and registration-correction rules and apply it to every county, report the sensitivity of the total to each assumption, and check the sum against cohort survival in the 1982 census microdata.
  • Archival series released in the 2000s, including the Song Yongyi document database, can be used to test county figures where both exist.

Existing work

  1. 1984
    Basil Ashton, Kenneth Hill, Alan Piazza and Robin Zeitz, Famine in China, 1958–61, Population and Development Review 10 (4): 613–645.First demographic reconstruction using newly released Chinese data; attributed close to 30 million premature deaths to the famine and documented a large fertility fall.
  2. 1984
    Ansley J. Coale, Rapid Population Change in China, 1952–1982, Washington, DC: National Academy Press (Committee on Population and Demography, Report 27).Used the 1982 census and 1982 fertility survey to reconstruct fertility and mortality since 1952, yielding an excess-death estimate of about 27 million.
  3. 1987
    Judith Banister, China's Changing Population, Stanford: Stanford University Press.Comprehensive demographic history of the PRC whose adjusted mortality series implied about 30 million famine deaths.
  4. 1987
    Xizhe Peng, Demographic Consequences of the Great Leap Forward in China's Provinces, Population and Development Review 13 (4): 639–670.Provincial-level estimates of excess mortality and fertility loss, showing wide variation between provinces and between rural and urban areas.
  5. 2003
    James Kai-sing Kung and Justin Yifu Lin, The Causes of China's Great Leap Famine, 1959–1961, Economic Development and Cultural Change 52 (1): 51–73.Used provincial mortality variation to test explanations of the famine, noting a range of 16.5 to 30 million excess deaths in the literature.
  6. 2005
    Cao Shuji 曹树基, Da jihuang: 1959–1961 nian de Zhongguo renkou 大饑荒：1959–1961年的中國人口, Hong Kong: Shidai guoji chuban youxian gongsi.Reconstructed excess deaths by prefecture from county-level census figures and about a thousand gazetteers, arriving at roughly 32.5 million.
  7. 2010
    Frank Dikötter, Mao's Great Famine: The History of China's Most Devastating Catastrophe, 1958–1962, London: Bloomsbury; New York: Walker.Drew on provincial and county party archives to argue for at least 45 million premature deaths over 1958–62, including deaths from violence.
  8. 2012
    Yang Jisheng, Tombstone: The Great Chinese Famine, 1958–1962, New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux (trans. of Mubei, Hong Kong: Tiandi tushu, 2008).Province-by-province account based on archives and interviews, estimating about 36 million deaths (link is the China Quarterly review).
  9. 2013
    Anthony Garnaut, Hard facts and half-truths: The new archival history of China's Great Famine, China Information 27 (2): 223–246.Critique of archival death-toll claims, especially Dikötter's, arguing that archival documents need the same source criticism as demographic data; Dikötter replied in the same journal.
  10. 2013
    Cormac Ó Gráda, Great Leap, Great Famine: A Review Essay, Population and Development Review 39 (2): 333–346.Comparative assessment of Dikötter's and Yang Jisheng's estimates against the demographic literature.
  11. 2015
    Xin Meng, Nancy Qian and Pierre Yared, The Institutional Causes of China's Great Famine, 1959–1961, Review of Economic Studies 82 (4): 1568–1611.Cited a range of 16.5 to 45 million deaths and showed that rural mortality was positively correlated with local food production, attributing this to inflexible procurement.
  12. 2020
    Hiroyuki Kasahara and Bingjing Li, Grain exports and the causes of China's Great Famine, 1959–1961: County-level evidence, Journal of Development Economics 146: 102513.Built a county panel of death rates for 1955–1963 from gazetteers and estimated that grain exports account for about 15 percent of excess deaths.
  13. 2021
    Songlin Yang, Telling the Truth: China's Great Leap Forward, Household Registration and the Famine Death Tally, Singapore: Palgrave Macmillan (trans. Baohui Xie).Revisionist argument that rectification of earlier under-registered deaths inflated recorded mortality in 1959–61 and that verifiable famine deaths were far fewer than 30 million.

Archives and collections

  • Universities Service Centre for China Studies Collection, Chinese University of Hong Kong Librarypartly digitized PRC county and provincial gazetteers (xinbian difangzhi), statistical yearbooks and provincial population compilationsMajor open-shelf collection of post-1980 local gazetteers and statistical materials used by most county-level famine studies; open to visiting researchers.
  • Fairbank Center for Chinese Studies, Harvard University, and Universities Service Centre, CUHKdigitized Song Yongyi (chief ed.), The Chinese Great Leap Forward–Great Famine Database (1958–1962), 2013Over 7,000 archival and internal documents on the Great Leap and famine; distributed to subscribing libraries (link is a profile of the editor at China Unofficial Archives).
  • IPUMS International, University of Minnesotadigitized China 1982 population census microdata sampleHarmonized census microdata allowing cohort-survival checks on births and deaths around 1959–61; free registration required.
  • Harvard-Yenching Library, Harvard University, Cambridge, MApartly digitized PRC-era county gazetteers and local statistical compilationsLarge holding of post-1980 xian zhi, many of which contain annual population, birth and death tables for the 1950s–60s.
  • China Unofficial Archives (Minjian Danganguan)partly digitized Digitized independent documentary collections on the Great Leap Forward and famineOpen online archive of unofficial and leaked documents, memoirs and reference works on the Maoist period.
  • China Historical GIS, Harvard University and Fudan Universitydigitized CHGIS county boundariesBoundary data needed to harmonize counties across the 1953, 1964 and 1982 censuses after administrative changes.
