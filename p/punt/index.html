Where was the land of Punt?

Egypt traded by sea with Punt for over a thousand years, for incense, ebony, gold and baboons, but whether it lay in Africa, Arabia or both remains disputed.

Places · Africa · 2500–1150 BCEscience

Why it matters

Punt is the best-documented long-distance partner of pharaonic Egypt, attested from the Fifth Dynasty (Sahure) to Ramesses III, and its position determines the scale of Bronze Age Red Sea navigation and the reach of Egyptian exchange into the Horn of Africa or southern Arabia. It bears on the origins of the incense trade, on the early history of the Gash Delta and Eritrean lowland cultures that some archaeologists identify as Puntite, and on how far the of Hatshepsut record observed people, houses, animals and plants rather than a stylized exotic land.

Why it is open

Egyptian texts name Punt and its products but give no distances or coastlines, and Punt itself left no texts that name it. The commodities (myrrh and frankincense, ebony, gold, baboons, leopard skins) occur on both sides of the southern Red Sea, so lists and images can be read either way, and proposals have ranged from coastal Sudan and Eritrea to Somalia and western Arabia. Archaeology of the second millennium BCE on both coasts is thin, and harbour finds at document Egyptian departure and return rather than the destination.

What would count as a solution

Independent provenance results for several classes of Punt imports (baboons, aromatic resins, obsidian, ebony, pottery) excavated in dated Egyptian contexts converge on a bounded region, and that region contains contemporary sites whose material culture matches the Puntite goods and ceramics found at .

Potential approach

  • Compile every Egyptian attestation of Punt and Puntites, with commodities, dates, routes and accompanying images, from published corpora and the Deir el-Bahari and Sahure reliefs, and tag each product with its known natural range on both coasts.
  • Assemble the isotope, ancient DNA and geochemical provenance results now published for baboons, obsidian and resins into one geographic dataset, and map where the evidence overlaps and where it has been inferred from a single specimen.
  • Identify museum objects likely to be Punt imports in datable contexts (New Kingdom baboon mummies, resin residues, ebony, obsidian from ) that have not yet been sampled.
  • Collaborate with primatologists and isotope geochemists to extend oxygen and strontium baselines and mitochondrial reference data for baboons in Eritrea, Sudan, Somalia and Yemen, and with organic chemists on residue analysis of resins against modern Boswellia and Commiphora reference samples.
  • Test the result against field data from Eritrean, Sudanese and Yemeni coastal sites of the second millennium BCE.

Existing work

  1. 1969
    D. M. Dixon, The Transplantation of Punt Incense Trees in Egypt, Journal of Egyptian Archaeology 55: 55–65.Examined the incense trees shown being shipped from Punt at Deir el-Bahari and their botanical identity, a key strand in arguments about where Punt lay.
  2. 1993
    K. A. Kitchen, The land of Punt, in T. Shaw, P. Sinclair, B. Andah and A. Okpoko (eds.), The Archaeology of Africa: Food, Metals and Towns. London: Routledge.Influential synthesis that used the fauna, flora and textual itineraries to place Punt on the African side, in the region of coastal Sudan and Eritrea and their hinterland.
  3. 1997
    Jacke Phillips, Punt and Aksum: Egypt and the Horn of Africa, Journal of African History 38 (3): 423–457.Reviewed the Egyptian and Horn of Africa evidence and the archaeological cultures of the Eritrean and Sudanese lowlands as candidates for Punt.
  4. 2003
    Dimitri Meeks, Locating Punt, in D. O'Connor and S. Quirke (eds.), Mysterious Lands. London: UCL Press: 53–80.Main modern statement of the Arabian hypothesis, arguing from the textual geography that Punt lay on the western coast of the Arabian peninsula.
  5. 2007
    Kathryn A. Bard and Rodolfo Fattovich (eds.), Harbor of the Pharaohs to the Land of Punt: Archaeological Investigations at Mersa/Wadi Gawasis, Egypt, 2001–2005, Naples: Università degli Studi di Napoli "L'Orientale".First report on the Middle Kingdom harbour, with inscribed stelae and ostraca naming Punt and Bia-Punt, ship timbers, cargo boxes and imported pottery.
  6. 2010
    Kathryn A. Bard and Rodolfo Fattovich, Spatial Use of the Twelfth Dynasty Harbor at Mersa/Wadi Gawasis for the Seafaring Expeditions to Punt, Journal of Ancient Egyptian Interconnections 2 (3).Described the shoreline shrines, storage caves and harbour layout and the dating of their use within the Twelfth Dynasty.
  7. 2012
    Rodolfo Fattovich, Egypt's trade with Punt: New discoveries on the Red Sea coast, British Museum Studies in Ancient Egypt and Sudan 18.Synthesis linking the Mersa/Wadi Gawasis finds to the Gash Group and related cultures of the Sudanese–Eritrean lowlands, which Fattovich identified as Puntite.
  8. 2016
    Francis Breyer, Punt: Die Suche nach dem "Gottesland", Leiden: Brill (Culture and History of the Ancient Near East 80).Comprehensive review of ancient and modern searches for Punt, with a full dossier of the Egyptian sources, arguing for an African location.
  9. 2018
    Kathryn A. Bard and Rodolfo Fattovich, Seafaring Expeditions to Punt in the Middle Kingdom: Excavations at Mersa/Wadi Gawasis, Egypt, Leiden: Brill.Final synthesis of the harbour excavations, including the long-distance routes and the evidence from the site for Punt's location in the southern Red Sea.
  10. 2020
    Julien Cooper, Toponymy on the Periphery: Placenames of the Eastern Desert, Red Sea, and South Sinai in Egyptian Documents from the Early Dynastic until the End of the New Kingdom, Leiden: Brill (Probleme der Ägyptologie).Systematic study of Egyptian placenames for the eastern deserts and Red Sea that situates Punt within the wider toponymy of the region.
  11. 2020
    Nathaniel J. Dominy, Salima Ikram, Gillian L. Moritz, Patrick V. Wheatley, John N. Christensen, Jonathan W. Chipman and Paul L. Koch, Mummified baboons reveal the far reach of early Egyptian mariners, eLife 9: e60860.Oxygen and strontium isotopes of two New Kingdom hamadryas baboon mummies in the British Museum pointed to a region spanning much of Ethiopia, Eritrea and Djibouti and parts of Somalia and Yemen.
  12. 2020
    Giulio Lucarini, Donatella Barca and Andrea Manzo, The provenance of obsidian artefacts from the Middle Kingdom harbour of Mersa/Wadi Gawasis, Egypt, and its implications for Red Sea trade routes in the 2nd millennium BC, Quaternary International 555: 85–95.LA-ICP-MS analysis matched obsidian from the harbour to sources in both Eritrea and Yemen, implying contacts with both shores of the southern Red Sea.
  13. 2023
    Franziska Grathwol, Christian Roos, Dietmar Zinner, Benjamin Hume, Stéphanie M. Porcier, Didier Berthet, Jacques Cuisin, Stefan Merker, Claudio Ottoni, Wim Van Neer, Nathaniel J. Dominy and Gisela H. Kopp, Adulis and the transshipment of baboons during classical antiquity, eLife 12: RP87513.Complete mitochondrial genome of a Late Period baboon mummy from Gabbanat el-Qurud (c. 800–540 BCE) placed it with populations in Eritrea and northeastern Sudan, which the authors linked to Adulis and, more speculatively, to Punt.
  14. 2023
    Wim Van Neer, Mircea Udrescu, Joris Peters, Bea De Cupere, Stéphane Pasquali and Stéphanie Porcier, Palaeopathological and demographic data reveal conditions of keeping of the ancient baboons at Gabbanat el-Qurud (Thebes, Egypt), PLOS ONE 18 (12): e0294934.Skeletal and demographic study suggesting a local breeding population founded from hamadryas baboons from the Horn of Africa or southern Arabia, a caution against reading every mummy as a direct import.
