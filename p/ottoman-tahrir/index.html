Did the population of Ottoman Anatolia collapse in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries?

Tax surveys show rapid growth in Anatolia to about 1580, but whether the decline that followed was a demographic collapse or an artefact of changing registration remains unsettled.

Demography · Middle East · 1520–1700digitization

Could use help from archivists and librarians, demographers and GIS specialists.

Why it matters

A large fall in Anatolian population would connect the Celali rebellions, the Little Ice Age droughts of the 1590s, rural flight and the transformation of Ottoman fiscal and military institutions into one crisis comparable to the 'general crisis' of seventeenth-century Europe and China. If the decline was smaller or mainly a shift from registered villages to towns, nomadism and untaxed settlements, then narratives of Ottoman 'decline' and of climate-driven collapse need revision. The question also tests whether Ottoman tax registers can be used for demography at all.

Why it is open

The detailed provincial surveys (tahrir defterleri) end in most of Anatolia by the 1580s and record taxable household heads rather than people, while the seventeenth-century avarız and cizye registers that follow count fiscal units (avarızhane) whose relation to households changed over time. Comparisons across the two series therefore mix demographic change with changes in what was counted. Most studies cover single districts, the registers are held in two archives with different access regimes, and many have never been transcribed.

What would count as a solution

A linked village-level dataset across all surviving tahrir and avarız registers for a large sample of Anatolian districts, with a validated model converting each register's fiscal units into households, that produces regional population series from 1520 to 1700 with uncertainty and that agrees with independent evidence such as court records, settlement abandonment and cizye counts.

Potential approach

  • The debate is already framed by decades of register work. (1957) and (1972) read the sixteenth-century tahrirs as evidence of strong growth and pressure on land, (1979) located a turning point around the 1580s, and (2004) and (2016) linked late tahrirs to seventeenth-century avarız registers to argue for a deep, lasting decline. (2011) tied that decline to drought, famine and the Celali rebellions described by (1963) and (1983).
  • Much of the evidence is already transcribed in published district editions, such as the Kayseri registers edited by (2009), and in monographs issued by the . Collaborative teams could structure these into a common table of villages, household heads, bachelors and exempt groups, with every figure traced to a page of a register.
  • The core difficulty is that tahrir and avarız registers count different things, as (1990), (1992), (1992) and (2004) emphasise. Where registers for the same district overlap, conversion factors between fiscal units and households can be estimated, and these should be carried forward as ranges rather than single multipliers.
  • Linking villages across registers and to modern locations would show where settlements were abandoned rather than merely re-registered. The nineteenth-century gazetteer of et al. (2022) offers a bridge between Ottoman toponyms and coordinates, and a mapped series for a few well-documented districts would be useful even if an empire-wide figure stays out of reach.
  • Two explanations then compete: real demographic collapse through violence, famine and flight, or an artefact of changing fiscal practice, with households hidden, merged into larger avarız units or moved outside the surveyed villages. Regional series can be tested against climate reconstructions, the timing of Celali violence and court-record evidence of flight and abandoned villages in 's copies of the kadı registers, which would tend to confirm collapse where all three move together.
  • Scaling beyond published registers requires systematic digitization of the and in the and the in Ankara, with archivists and Ottoman palaeographers. Many registers use siyakat numerals and chancery hands that need specialist verification, and automated transcription has so far been tested mainly on print, as in (2022).

Who can help

archivists and librarians
Locate and transcribe tahrir, avarız and cizye registers for the same districts in the TT.d, MAD.d and KK.d series, tracing every figure to a register page.
demographers
Estimate conversion ranges between avarızhane and households where tahrir and avarız registers overlap for a district, and build regional population series from 1520 to 1700 with uncertainty.
GIS specialists
Link villages across registers to modern coordinates through the gazetteer of Kabadayı et al. (2022), and map which settlements were abandoned rather than re-registered.
