When did the thylacine go extinct in the wild?

The last known thylacine died in a Hobart zoo in September 1936, but whether the species survived in the Tasmanian bush into the 1940s, the 1980s or later remains disputed.

Chronology · Pacific · 1888–2019digitizationscience

Could use help from statisticians and data scientists, archivists and librarians, geneticists and GIS specialists.

Why it matters

The thylacine is the standard example of a historically documented mammal extinction caused by colonial persecution, and its date bears on how historians weigh the government bounty of 1888 to 1909, private bounties from the 1830s, habitat change and possible disease. Whether it vanished by the 1940s or lingered into the 1980s also decides whether the protection granted in 1936 and the reserves of the 1960s and 1970s came too late by years or by decades. Beyond Tasmania, it is the main test case for methods that infer extinction dates from a mix of specimens and unverified sightings, which conservation bodies use to declare species extinct.

Why it is open

Absence leaves no document, so the evidence after 1930 consists almost entirely of sightings, whose reliability is hard to judge and which continued every year through the twentieth century. Estimates therefore depend on which reports are admitted: , Bond and Burgio (2018) put the most likely date near 1940, while Brook and colleagues (2023), using a larger graded database, found that the full record allows extinction as late as the 1980s to early 2000s. Even the anchoring events are contested: the last confirmed wild kill is usually given as Wilf Batty's at Mawbanna in 1930, and (2023) and , Williams and Holmes (2020) disagree about when and where the last captive animals were taken.

What would count as a solution

A public, versioned database of all post-1910 thylacine records with a reliability grading that has been validated against reports of known accuracy, together with an extinction-date estimate whose credible interval and sensitivity to that grading are reported; or physical evidence such as a specimen, DNA from a securely dated sample, or a verified photograph that moves the last confirmed date. The most likely outcome is a credible interval rather than a single year.

Potential approach

  • The human literature gives the baselines: (1985) and (2000) on bounties, decline and the 1930s; (2015) on post-1900 range; and the sighting compilations behind , Bond and Burgio (2018) and Brook and colleagues (2023), whose Tasmanian Thylacine Sighting Record Database lists 1,223 rated records from 1910 to 2019.
  • Teams could text-mine 's digitized Tasmanian newspapers and transcribe the wildlife-service sighting files held by and the , matching each report to existing database entries to find unrecorded sightings and duplicates, and recording who saw what, where and in what conditions.
  • Reliability grading should be made explicit and repeatable: a coding scheme applied by several independent coders, with agreement measured, and calibrated on comparable reports of animals whose presence is known, such as sightings of foxes or quolls later confirmed or refuted.
  • Extinction-date estimators, including the (1993) family and the re-sampling method of Brook, Buettel and Jarić (2019), should first be tested on species with known extinction or rediscovery dates, as (2003) did for the dodo, before results are reported for the thylacine with their sensitivity to grading and to observer effort.
  • An independent check needs field scientists: environmental DNA sampling and camera-trap surveys in the south-western wilderness, where Brook and colleagues (2023) left a small chance of late persistence, and DNA and radiocarbon testing of any hair, scat or skin samples with a post-1930 provenance.

Who can help

statisticians and data scientists
Test extinction-date estimators that use graded sightings on species with known extinction or rediscovery dates, and report how the thylacine estimate shifts with each grading scheme.
archivists and librarians
Identify and list the Tasmanian Archives, wildlife-service and museum files holding post-1910 thylacine reports, bounty payments and zoo records, and prioritize the undigitized series for scanning.
geneticists
Design an environmental DNA survey of the south-western Tasmanian wilderness with stated detection limits, and test whether dated post-1930 hair, scat or skin samples contain thylacine DNA.
GIS specialists
Geolocate every graded sighting against road, track, settlement and land-clearance layers by decade, so that apparent refugia can be separated from where observers happened to be.
