Whose voices survive on unidentified early wax cylinders?

Early recordings preserve speakers whose names are missing or disputed. Can the words on the cylinders and contemporary records identify them?

Why it matters

Putting a name to a voice can recover who used the phonograph, who was meant to hear it, and how private speech and public performance first entered recorded sound.

Why it is open

Surface noise obscures names and announcements. Labels can be incomplete, and a cylinder may carry traces of an earlier recording. The probable identification of Frederick Dent Grant on EDIS 93953 provides a case to test.

What would count as a solution

A proposed identification should fit several independent clues or match a named recording of the same performance, with enough evidence for another listener to check it.

Potential approach

  • Start with short passages that name a place, person or sketch. Compare untouched transfers and documented restorations against family papers, programmes, trade catalogues and named recordings.
