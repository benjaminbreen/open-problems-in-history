Were real wages in the Yangzi delta and Bengal comparable to England's before 1750?

Whether the richest regions of China and India matched England before 1750 depends on wage series built from a few scattered price and wage quotations.

Economy · Global · 1500–1850digitization

Could use help from economists, archivists and librarians and statisticians and data scientists.

Why it matters

The date of the Great Divergence decides between rival explanations of why industrialisation began in north-western Europe. If the Yangzi delta and Bengal matched England until about 1750 or 1800, as and Parthasarathi argued, the divergence is best explained by late and contingent factors such as coal and access to New World land and markets; if they had fallen behind by 1600 or 1700, longer-run explanations based on institutions, demography or innovation gain ground. The answer also shapes judgments about the economic effects of Qing rule and of East India Company rule in Bengal after 1757.

Why it is open

The Chinese and Indian real-wage series rest on comparatively few quotations, often for different occupations, places and decades, drawn from construction regulations, merchant account books, gazetteers and European company records. Wages paid partly in kind, uncertain days worked per year, multiple monies (silver by weight, copper cash, rupees of varying standard) and local units of grain make each observation hard to convert. Results also move with the choice of consumption basket and deflator, so silver wages, grain wages and welfare ratios have supported different conclusions from much the same evidence, and critics such as question whether the data can bear the comparison at all.

What would count as a solution

Welfare-ratio series for the Yangzi delta and for Bengal, each built from at least two independent record types that agree within stated error, decade by decade from 1600 to 1800, with the point at which they fall below England located within a stated interval that holds under alternative baskets and deflators.

Potential approach

  • The positions are well defined. (2000) and (1998, 2011) argued that the advanced regions of Asia matched England until the later eighteenth century; (2006) answered that grain wages were similar but silver wages much lower, and et al. (2011) placed eighteenth-century Chinese building workers alongside the poorer parts of Europe. (2009), and van Zanden (2012) and Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) compared the Yangzi delta directly with England and the Netherlands, while (2010), (2009) and and Lucassen (2020) did the same for Bengal and other Indian regions.
  • The first step is a single, citable register of every wage and price quotation already used, linked to its archival source, with original units and currencies recorded. (2016) showed how thin and heterogeneous the Chinese base is, and the files gathered by the and the archive, including the Qing wage regulations, are the obvious starting point.
  • AI research agents could then extract new quotations at scale from records that are digitized but not tabulated: prices from the and the in the , wages and purchases in , and payments to labourers, weavers and boatmen in the , and factory diaries and in the 's now being transcribed by . Every conversion of units and monies should be stated and reversible.
  • Independent series should then be tested against each other: official construction wages against wages paid in merchant account books, company wages against Mughal and regional price data, and grain-price trends against silver-copper exchange rates. (2023) extended Chinese wages back to 1530 by combining sources, and the disagreement between (2025) and Broadberry, Guan and Li over error margins shows why uncertainty must be modelled explicitly.
  • With the series in hand, the crossover with England can be estimated under the respectability and bare-bones baskets, with and without climate and household adjustments, to see whether the date is robust. Progress depends on wider imaging of Huizhou account books and Bengal factory records and on economic historians who read classical Chinese, Persian, Bengali and early modern Dutch checking extracted values before they enter the series.

Who can help

economists
Rebuild Yangzi delta and Bengal welfare ratios decade by decade for 1600 to 1800 under several baskets and deflators, reporting where each places the crossover with England.
archivists and librarians
Identify and image the Huizhou account books and Bengal factory diaries that record wages and prices, with item-level finding aids so each quotation can be traced.
statisticians and data scientists
Fit a model that pools wage and price quotations of different kinds, places and units, and propagate measurement and conversion uncertainty into each decade's welfare ratio.

Existing work

  1. 1998
    Prasannan Parthasarathi, Rethinking Wages and Competitiveness in the Eighteenth Century: Britain and South India, Past & Present 158: 79–109.Argued that the grain earnings of South Indian weavers and labourers were equal to or above those of their British counterparts in the eighteenth century.
  2. 2000
    Kenneth Pomeranz, The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy, Princeton: Princeton University Press.Founding statement of the late-divergence case, arguing that the Yangzi delta and England had comparable living standards until about 1750 and diverged mainly after 1800 through coal and colonies.
  3. 2006
    Stephen Broadberry and Bishnupriya Gupta, The Early Modern Great Divergence: Wages, Prices and Economic Development in Europe and Asia, 1500–1800, Economic History Review 59 (1): 2–31.Found grain wages in advanced India and China comparable to north-western Europe but silver wages far lower, and argued that divergence was under way before 1800.
  4. 2009
    Robert C. Allen, Agricultural Productivity and Rural Incomes in England and the Yangtze Delta, c.1620–c.1820, Economic History Review 62 (3): 525–550.Estimated Yangzi output per day worked at about 90 per cent of England's c. 1820 and peasant family income similar to an English farm labourer's, but falling since 1620.
  5. 2009
    Sashi Sivramkrishna, Ascertaining Living Standards in Erstwhile Mysore, Southern India, from Francis Buchanan's Journey of 1800–01: An Empirical Contribution to the Great Divergence Debate, Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient 52 (4): 695–733.Assembled the prices and wages scattered through Buchanan's survey into an aggregate welfare ratio for Mysore on the eve of colonial rule.
  6. 2010
    Tirthankar Roy, Economic Conditions in Early Modern Bengal: A Contribution to the Divergence Debate, Journal of Economic History 70 (1): 179–194.Estimated that average income in pre-colonial Bengal was well below western Europe's and that the evidence cannot show growth or decline across the eighteenth century.
  7. 2011
    Robert C. Allen, Jean-Pascal Bassino, Debin Ma, Christine Moll-Murata and Jan Luiten van Zanden, Wages, Prices, and Living Standards in China, 1738–1925: In Comparison with Europe, Japan, and India, Economic History Review 64 (S1): 8–38.Built wage and price series for Beijing, Canton and Suzhou/Shanghai and found eighteenth-century real wages like those of backward Europe and far below London and Amsterdam.
  8. 2011
    Prasannan Parthasarathi, Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not: Global Economic Divergence, 1600–1850, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Argued from the Indian case that advanced Europe and Asia were alike until the later eighteenth century and attributed divergence to competitive and ecological pressures.
  9. 2012
    Bozhong Li and Jan Luiten van Zanden, Before the Great Divergence? Comparing the Yangzi Delta and the Netherlands at the Beginning of the Nineteenth Century, Journal of Economic History 72 (4): 956–989.Found Dutch GDP per head almost twice that of part of the Yangzi delta in the 1820s, with similar agricultural productivity but large gaps in industry and services.
  10. 2015
    Stephen Broadberry, Johann Custodis and Bishnupriya Gupta, India and the Great Divergence: An Anglo-Indian Comparison of GDP per Capita, 1600–1871, Explorations in Economic History 55: 58–75.Historical national accounts for India from 1600, offering an output-based check on wage-based comparisons with Britain.
  11. 2016
    Kent Deng and Patrick O'Brien, Establishing Statistical Foundations of a Chronology for the Great Divergence: A Survey and Critique of the Primary Sources for the Construction of Relative Wage Levels for Ming–Qing China, Economic History Review 69 (4): 1057–1082.Surveyed the Chinese sources behind real-wage comparisons and judged much of the evidence to lie between guesswork and plausible conjecture.
  12. 2018
    Stephen Broadberry, Hanhui Guan and David Daokui Li, China, Europe, and the Great Divergence: A Study in Historical National Accounting, 980–1850, Journal of Economic History 78 (4): 955–1000.GDP per head estimates for China from 980 that date the divergence earlier than the California School proposed but later than older Eurocentric accounts; a 2021 restatement placed its start around 1700.
  13. 2020
    Pim de Zwart and Jan Lucassen, Poverty or Prosperity in Northern India? New Evidence on Real Wages, 1590s–1870s, Economic History Review 73 (3): 644–667.New wage dataset from Gujarat to Bengal, adjusted for climate and body size, dating the divergence to the late seventeenth century with widening after the 1720s.
  14. 2023
    Ziang Liu, Wages, Labour Markets, and Living Standards in China, 1530–1840, Explorations in Economic History 92: 101569.Long-run Chinese wage series showing real-wage falls in the 1620s–1650s and 1740s–1760s and a Lower Yangzi–England gap that widens after 1700.
