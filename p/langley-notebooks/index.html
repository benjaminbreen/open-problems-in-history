What is in Samuel P. Langley’s 37 shorthand notebooks?

The Smithsonian holds 37 notebooks from an early experimenter in flight. Reading their shorthand could reveal who proposed a design, changed it or saw it fail.

Science & knowledge · Americas · 1898–1902decipherment

Could use help from archivists and librarians and philologists and epigraphers.

What do James Wilkinson's unread coded dispatches to Spanish officials say? →Whose voices survive on unidentified early wax cylinders? →

Why it matters

Samuel Pierpont studied the Sun, ran the Smithsonian and built experimental flying machines. Ordinary handwriting in records Alexander Graham Bell suggesting changes to a kite. Measurements in Bell’s own papers place the conversation at a March 1902 outing. The shorthand could preserve other exchanges that the published accounts passed over.

Why it is open

The 37 notebooks, digitized in 7,248 scans, mix shorthand with longhand, lists and diagrams. Some entries concern an aquarium. A memorandum names George Wells as a stenographer. Who wrote each book, and which shorthand system they used, still need checking.

What would count as a solution

Identify the system and hands from repeated, independently readable passages. A dated guide to the volumes and a checked transcription of selected entries, with uncertain strokes marked, would let readers judge what the notebooks add.

Potential approach

  • Start with pages that alternate shorthand and longhand. Compare the same names, dates and phrases against contemporary shorthand manuals.
  • Ask two readers to transcribe unseen pages independently and record which words they can recover reliably.
  • Link each reading to its scan and test proposed dates and names against correspondence, including Bell’s account of the kite trials.
