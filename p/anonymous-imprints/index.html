Who printed the anonymous and false-imprint books of eighteenth-century Britain?

Many thousands of British books and pamphlets of 1695 to 1800 name no printer or a fictitious one; ornaments and damaged type can now identify the presses at scale.

Texts & authorship · Europe · 1695–1800digitization

Could use help from archivists and librarians, statisticians and data scientists and conservation scientists.

Why it matters

After the Licensing Act lapsed in 1695, prepublication censorship gave way to prosecution after the fact for seditious, blasphemous and obscene libel, and printers and booksellers hid their part in risky work behind blank imprints, the names of trade publishers and invented names such as "A. Moore". Who actually printed this material decides how far opposition journalism, Jacobite and dissenting polemic, ministerial propaganda and erotica came from the same shops that printed sermons and law books, and whether particular printers specialised in concealed work. It also matters for the economics of the trade, the share of output that escaped the Stationers' registers and the risk the courts placed on printers, and for attributing anonymous texts to authors, which often begins with the shop that set them.

Why it is open

Imprints are unreliable in several different ways. Some books carry only "Printed in the Year", some name trade publishers or mercuries (real distributors such as John Morphew, J. Roberts or Anne Dodd who fronted for the owners of a copy), and some invent names outright, so the name on the title page rarely identifies the press. Ornament evidence has been gathered one printer at a time, in stock catalogues for the Bowyers and Henry Woodfall and in studies of Richardson and a few others, while most London and provincial printers have no catalogue of their blocks at all. Until large image corpora became searchable, matching an ornament in an anonymous pamphlet against the output of every other shop was impractical, and even a match does not settle the question, because blocks were copied, lent and sold, sheets of one book could be farmed out to several houses, and microfilm-derived images blur the damage that distinguishes one block from its copy.

What would count as a solution

For a defined corpus, such as every item of 1700 to 1760 with no printer named or a printer's name known to be fictitious, each item is either assigned to a printing house or recorded as unattributed with the reason. An assignment counts only if it rests on physical identity of type or blocks (shared damage or wear, not similar design), from at least two independent ornaments or damaged sorts, or one plus documentary evidence, checked sheet by sheet and dated against the printer's signed output. The same rule must be shown to give a stated low error rate on a control set of books whose printers are known.

Potential approach

  • The method has a long manual history to build on: Sale (1950) on Richardson's ornaments, the stock catalogues of (1973) and (1988), the printer attributions in (1975), and (1982) on trade publishers. (2016) showed that the "A. Moore" imprint concealed several different shops, while (2024) used waste sheets in to name one bookseller behind the name, which identifies a publisher rather than a printer.
  • , built from the page images by Wilkinson, Briggs and Gorissen (2021), already offers visual search across more than a million extracted ornaments. Research agents could run every ornament in the target corpus against it, keep only matches to books with a signed printer's imprint, and record for each candidate the gathering in which the ornament falls, so that shared printing by sheet shows up instead of being averaged away.
  • Ornaments alone are a weak test because designs were copied and blocks changed hands, and et al. (2026) find headpiece reuse networks far more complex than one block to one owner. Damaged type is a second, independent line: the project's work on Areopagitica, the attribution of the Leviathan "Ornaments" edition in et al. (2021), and a contrastive type-matching model published by the same group in 2023 show how distinctive sorts can be matched, and their catalogue covers London printing to 1700. Extending that catalogue into the eighteenth century would let the two kinds of evidence check each other.
  • Each attribution can be checked on its own, so the work can proceed book by book. A public table would list each item's number, the matched ornaments and sorts with image links, the anchoring signed books, any documentary evidence such as ledger entries in the , and the stated confidence, with failed and contradictory matches kept.
  • Many ECCO images derive from bitonal microfilm, and different digital copies often descend from the same film, so they are not independent witnesses. Confirming that two impressions come from one block needs new high-resolution imaging of specific original copies, a request that rare-book libraries such as the , Bodleian and Folger can fulfil for a short list of decisive pages.

Who can help

archivists and librarians
Index printing-house ledgers, State Papers and Treasury Solicitor libel files by title and date so each ornament attribution can be checked against a payment, warrant or examination.
statisticians and data scientists
Estimate false-match rates for ornament and damaged-type identifications on books with known printers, and set an evidence threshold that keeps wrong attributions below a stated rate.
conservation scientists
Image selected copies at high resolution under raking light, measuring ornament impressions and cracks to tell one physical block from a close copy.

  1. 1950
    William M. Sale Jr., Samuel Richardson: Master Printer, Cornell Studies in English 37. Ithaca: Cornell University Press.Reconstructed Richardson's output as a printer largely from the ornaments in his books, and set the model for attributing unsigned work by ornament stock.
  2. 1973
    K. I. D. Maslen, The Bowyer Ornament Stock, Oxford Bibliographical Society Occasional Publication 8. Oxford.Illustrated catalogue of the Bowyers' ornaments, which can be checked against the surviving Bowyer printing ledgers.
  3. 1975
    D. F. Foxon, English Verse 1701–1750: A Catalogue of Separately Printed Poems with Notes on Contemporary Collected Editions, London and New York: Cambridge University Press.Standard catalogue of verse pamphlets that records printer attributions for many anonymous and falsely imprinted items, often on ornament evidence.
  4. 1982
    Michael Treadwell, London Trade Publishers 1675–1750, The Library, 6th ser., 4 (2): 99–134.Defined the trade publisher, whose name on an imprint marked distribution rather than ownership or printing, and showed why such imprints mislead attribution.
  5. 1988
    R. J. Goulden, The Ornament Stock of Henry Woodfall, 1719–1747: A Preliminary Inventory, Occasional Papers of the Bibliographical Society 3. London: The Bibliographical Society.Inventory of a major London printer's blocks, one of the few reference stocks against which unsigned work can be matched.
  6. 2013
    Hazel Wilkinson, Printers' Flowers as Evidence in the Identification of Unknown Printers: Two Examples from 1715, The Library, 7th ser., 14 (1): 70–79.Identified John Darby as printer of two 1715 books by showing that composed arrangements of type flowers were kept standing and reused across jobs.
  7. 2014
    John D. Gordan III, John Nutt: Trade Publisher and Printer 'In the Savoy', The Library, 7th ser., 15 (3): 243–260.Career of a man who was first a trade publisher and then a printer, illustrating how the two roles behind an imprint must be kept apart.
  8. 2016
    Hazel Wilkinson, Benjamin Franklin's London Printing 1725–26, Papers of the Bibliographical Society of America 110 (2): 139–180.Assigned most of the items in an appendix of work from the Palmer and Watts printing houses to those shops for the first time, combining archival and digital evidence.
