Do the missing voices of Renaissance songs survive in lute arrangements?

Printers sold each voice of a song in a separate book. Where only one part survives, a lute player’s arrangement might let us hear the voices together again.

Texts & authorship · Europe · 1500–1600

Could use help from musicologists and archivists and librarians.

Why it matters

A lute player could play several parts on one instrument, writing down where to put the fingers on its strings. These books of fingerings, called tablatures, can preserve a version of a song whose singers’ books are now incomplete.

Why it is open

Royston Gustavson and David Fallows rediscovered a tenor book in Bern from a set Christian Egenolff printed in 1536. ReRenaissance and the RISM brought nine pieces to recording in 2025. A lute arrangement might preserve additional notes, but players also added ornaments, left out voices and changed harmonies.

What would count as a solution

Match several distinctive phrases from the surviving vocal line in order, then have a reader compare the entire arrangement with the tenor and other surviving sources. Credit the result as a historical arrangement. Attribute a particular note to a lost vocal part only where another witness supports it.

Potential approach

  • Begin with songs that survive both in vocal partbooks and in dated lute tablatures. Hide the titles and test how often the search recovers the known arrangements.
  • Search for phrases from the Bern tenor in other tablatures, allowing for transposition, added notes and changed titles. Compare early collections with arrangements made after 1536.
  • Publish the two readings side by side so musicians can see which notes survive, which the arranger supplied and which a modern performance reconstructs.
