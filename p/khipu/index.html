Do khipus record more than numbers, and can their non-numerical signs be read?

Andean knotted cords encode numbers in a known decimal system; whether colour, ply, attachment and knot direction also record names, categories or narrative remains open.

Languages & scripts · Americas · 1400–1800digitizationdecipherment

Why it matters

Colonial chroniclers reported that khipukamayuq read histories, genealogies and laws from cords. If khipus encoded non-numerical information systematically, the Inka state kept records that could be read as primary sources for Andean history before and after 1532, and the comparative history of writing would need to include a non-graphic system. If they did not, accounts of Inka administration must explain how a large empire functioned on numerical records plus memory.

Why it is open

Roughly a thousand khipus survive (estimates vary), most without archaeological context, and very few can be paired with a Spanish-language transcription of the same record. The decimal knot system was established by (1912) and the Aschers, but colour, fibre, spin and ply, attachment direction and knot orientation vary in ways that have resisted consistent interpretation. Data were long scattered across incompatible recording schemes and unpublished notebooks.

What would count as a solution

A model trained on matched khipu–document pairs predicts, for khipus or transcriptions held out from training, non-numerical features (such as moiety, lineage, place or personal-name categories) significantly better than chance and better than numerical-only baselines, and the predictions are confirmed when new matched pairs are found.

Potential approach

  • Build on the structural studies of (1912), (2005) and (2005), the ethnography of (2004), and the colonial transcriptions gathered in (2004). Recent work has opened the non-numerical question directly: (2017) on , (2019) on paired signs at Inkawasi, and (2018) on the Santa Valley khipus.
  • Use the and the as a common schema. (2024) shows how far the arithmetic regularities of the corpus can be characterised at scale, which sets a baseline any non-numerical reading must go beyond.
  • Search colonial visitas, revisitas and court records in Peruvian and Spanish archives, including the , for documents stating that their figures were read from khipus. Each candidate pair, matched by totals and structure, is a small key; a vetted list of such pairs would be useful whatever it yields for decipherment.
  • Test rival readings against held-out cord groups, for example the alternative moiety and hierarchy alignments of Medrano (2018) and (2024). The deciding evidence is whether a proposed meaning for colour, ply or attachment predicts unseen cords better than chance.
  • Expect a mixed answer: some signs may prove to be category or name markers, while narrative khipus such as those at Collata stay only partly read.
  • Andeanists, khipu specialists and the communities that hold khipus should review every proposed reading. The work depends on recording undocumented khipus in museum stores, especially in , and smaller Peruvian collections, and on agreements with communities such as Collata and Rapaz.

Existing work

  1. 1912
    L. Leland Locke, The Ancient Quipu, a Peruvian Knot Record, American Anthropologist 14 (2): 325–332.Showed that knot types and positions encode a base-ten place-value system, the foundation of all later khipu reading.
  2. 2002
    Jeffrey Quilter and Gary Urton (eds.), Narrative Threads: Accounting and Recounting in Andean Khipu, Austin: University of Texas Press.Collected essays, including contributions by Marcia Ascher and William Conklin, on whether khipus could carry narrative as well as accounts.
  3. 2004
    Frank Salomon, The Cord Keepers: Khipus and Cultural Life in a Peruvian Village, Durham, NC: Duke University Press.Ethnography and archival history of the patrimonial khipus of Tupicocha, linking cords to colonial and later village records.
  4. 2004
    Martti Pärssinen and Jukka Kiviharju, Textos andinos: Corpus de textos khipu incaicos y coloniales, Madrid: Instituto Iberoamericano de Finlandia and Universidad Complutense, Tomo I (2004), Tomo II (2010).Edition of Spanish transcriptions of khipu-based testimony from Inca and colonial documents.
  5. 2005
    Gary Urton and Carrie J. Brezine, Khipu Accounting in Ancient Peru, Science 309 (5737): 1065–1067.Used the Khipu Database to identify hierarchical summation across a set of Puruchuco khipus and proposed that a recurring knot sequence might identify the place Puruchuco.
  6. 2005
    Marcia Ascher, How Can Spin, Ply, and Knot Direction Contribute to Understanding the Quipu Code?, Latin American Antiquity 16 (1): 99–111.Assessed whether construction features beyond knot value pattern consistently, drawing on the Aschers' decades of recorded khipu data.
  7. 2014
    Sabine Hyland, Gene A. Ware and Madison Clark, Knot Direction in a Khipu/Alphabetic Text from the Central Andes, Latin American Antiquity 25 (2): 189–197.Examined knot direction in a khipu paired with an alphabetic text, arguing that it carried non-numerical information.
  8. 2017
    Sabine Hyland, Writing with Twisted Cords: The Inscriptive Capacity of Andean Khipus, Current Anthropology 58 (3): 412–419.Analyzed two non-numerical khipus held by the community of San Juan de Collata and argued that their combinations of fibre, colour and ply direction are numerous enough to function as a logosyllabic sign system.
  9. 2018
    Manuel Medrano and Gary Urton, Toward the Decipherment of a Set of Mid-Colonial Khipus from the Santa Valley, Coastal Peru, Ethnohistory 65 (1): 1–23.Matched six khipus to a 1670 revisita of San Pedro de Corongo listing 132 named tributaries in six ayllus; knot totals equal the assessed tribute, and attachment direction appears to mark moiety.
  10. 2019
    Jon Clindaniel, Toward a Grammar of the Inka Khipu: Investigating the Production of Non-numerical Signs, PhD dissertation, Harvard University.Argues that non-numerical signs in Inkawasi and other khipus worked as paired, conventionalized predicates, and proposes a preliminary grammar.
  11. 2021
    Sabine Hyland, Sarah Bennison and William P. Hyland, Khipus, Khipu Boards, and Sacred Texts: Toward a Philology of Andean Knotted Cords, Latin American Research Review 56 (2): 400–416.Traced hybrid khipu/alphabetic 'khipu boards' from colonial Peru to twentieth-century ritual use at San Pedro de Casta.
  12. 2024
    Mackinley FitzPatrick, New Insights on Cord Attachment and Social Hierarchy in Six Khipus from the Santa Valley, Peru, Ethnohistory 71 (4): 443–469.Re-examined the Santa Valley match, identified gaps in its documentation, and used combinatorics to propose a different moiety alignment with recto and verso attachments as marked and unmarked signs.
  13. 2024
    Manuel Medrano and Ashok Khosla, How Can Data Science Contribute to Understanding the Khipu Code?, Latin American Antiquity 36, no. 2 (2025): 497-516; online 2024.Computational analysis of about 650 khipus finding that Ascher's sum relations characterize at least 74% of the corpus.
  14. 2025
    OKR Team (Open Khipu Research Laboratory; advisory board Carrie Brezine, Mackinley FitzPatrick, Iván Ghezzi, Sabine Hyland, Manuel Medrano, Jeffrey Splitstoser), The Open Khipu Repository, v2.1.0, Zenodo dataset.Open SQLite database of khipu cord-level data recorded through 2017 plus later archival submissions, with stable KH identifiers (KH0001–KH0702 as of December 2025).
  15. 2026
    Mackinley FitzPatrick, Knot Tricks: What Mathematical Knot Theory Can Reveal about the Structure of Khipu Knot Encoding, Latin American Antiquity 37 (2): 226–242.Formalized khipu knot types with knot theory, showing that some visually distinct knots are topologically equivalent and flagging common misidentifications in recorded data.
