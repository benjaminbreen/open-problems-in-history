How many died of influenza in India in 1918–19, and why was mortality so high?

India probably lost more lives than any other country in the 1918–19 pandemic, but estimates run from about 12 to over 17 million and the causes remain debated.

Disease · South Asia · 1918–1920digitization

Why it matters

Because India's toll is the largest single component of every global estimate, the uncertainty there drives much of the spread in estimates of world pandemic mortality, from under 20 million to 50 million or more. The causes of Indian mortality (concurrent drought and food scarcity, wartime prices, malaria, the state of colonial public health, age and sex patterns) are central to arguments about how poverty and governance shape pandemic outcomes and are used to model future pandemics. The demographic shock also figures in the history of Indian nationalism, of the 1921 census 'great divide' in population growth, and of colonial medicine.

Why it is open

Death registration in British India relied on village watchmen and was incomplete, and the princely states, covering roughly a fifth of the population, kept poorer records or none. Estimates have therefore depended on intercensal comparison, on extrapolation from better-registered provinces, or on assumptions about baseline mortality, which produce different totals. The district-level registers and sanitary reports that would allow finer analysis are held in provincial and state archives and are mostly undigitized.

What would count as a solution

A district-level panel of registered deaths for 1915–1921 covering all provinces of British India, with a documented correction for under-registration and an imputation for princely states, that yields an all-India excess mortality estimate with uncertainty bounds and identifies the district-level covariates that explain its variation.

Potential approach

  • Agents can transcribe the printed annual provincial sanitary and vital statistics reports and the 1921 census tables, already partly digitized, into a weekly or monthly district panel, and link them to rainfall, price and food-scarcity series and to malaria returns.
  • Models of excess mortality can then be tested against independent intercensal estimates.
  • Extending coverage to the princely states and to sub-district registers requires digitization of holdings in the National Archives of India and state archives, in collaboration with archivists there.

Existing work

  1. 1986
    I. D. Mills, The 1918–1919 Influenza Pandemic: The Indian Experience, Indian Economic and Social History Review 23 (1): 1–40.First detailed historical study; reviewed the official returns and Kingsley Davis's 1951 census-based estimate and discussed famine conditions and colonial administration as causes of high mortality.
  2. 1991
    K. David Patterson and Gerald F. Pyle, The geography and mortality of the 1918 influenza pandemic, Bulletin of the History of Medicine 65 (1): 4–21.Revised global mortality to 24.7–39.3 million from country estimates in which India was the largest component.
  3. 2002
    Niall P. A. S. Johnson and Juergen Mueller, Updating the Accounts: Global Mortality of the 1918–1920 'Spanish' Influenza Pandemic, Bulletin of the History of Medicine 76 (1): 105–115.Raised the global estimate to the order of 50 million, noting it could be understated by as much as 100 percent because of non-registration in countries such as India.
  4. 2006
    Christopher J. L. Murray, Alan D. Lopez, Brian Chin, Dennis Feehan and Kenneth H. Hill, Estimation of potential global pandemic influenza mortality on the basis of vital registry data from the 1918–20 pandemic: a quantitative analysis, The Lancet 368 (9554): 2211–2218.Used vital registration including Indian provinces to show large variation in excess mortality between populations, strongly associated with per-capita income.
  5. 2012
    Siddharth Chandra, Goran Kuljanin and Jennifer Wray, Mortality From the Influenza Pandemic of 1918–1919: The Case of India, Demography 49 (3): 857–865.Panel models on census data put deaths in the sampled districts of British India at no more than 13.88 million, against 17.21 million under Davis's assumptions, implying Davis overstated mortality by at least 24 percent.
  6. 2014
    Siddharth Chandra and Eva Kassens-Noor, The evolution of pandemic influenza: evidence from India, 1918–19, BMC Infectious Diseases 14: 510.Used weekly provincial mortality data to trace the spread of the epidemic across India and changes in its severity over time.
  7. 2018
    Peter Spreeuwenberg, Madelon Kroneman and John Paget, Reassessing the Global Mortality Burden of the 1918 Influenza Pandemic, American Journal of Epidemiology 187 (12): 2561–2567.Re-estimated mortality country by country and proposed a much lower global total of about 17 million, which makes the Indian figure even more decisive for the world total.
  8. 2019
    David Arnold, Death and the Modern Empire: The 1918–19 Influenza Epidemic in India, Transactions of the Royal Historical Society 29: 181–200.Argued that at least 12 million died and that the epidemic left little trace in official records because it was overshadowed by plague, wartime recruitment, and food shortages and price rises that brought India to the brink of famine.

Archives and collections

  • British Library, Londonpartly digitized India Office Records, official publications series (IOR/V), including annual reports of the Sanitary Commissioner with the Government of India and provincial sanitary and vital statistics reports, 1915–1922The printed provincial reports give monthly and district-level registered deaths; the report for 1918 contains a special account of the influenza epidemic.
  • National Archives of India, New Delhipartly digitized Home Department (Sanitary and Medical) and Department of Education, Health and Lands proceedings, 1918–1920Proceedings and correspondence on the epidemic, relief and reporting; the Abhilekh Patal portal lists and partly images the files.
  • Center for Research Libraries and JSTORdigitized South Asia Open Archives (SAOA)Open-access digitized census reports, gazetteers and government publications from colonial India, including material for 1911 and 1921.
  • Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner, Indiapartly digitized Census of India 1921 reports and tablesCensus volumes with provincial and district population counts, the basis of intercensal mortality estimates.
  • Tamil Nadu Archives, Chennainot digitized Madras Presidency Public Health and Local Self-Government proceedings, district sanitary reports, 1918–1920Example of state-held district-level material; comparable holdings exist in the Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal state archives. The website may not be reachable from outside India.
  • Digital South Asia Library, University of Chicagodigitized Statistical abstracts for British India, gazetteers and mapsOpen digitized statistical series useful for district covariates such as prices, rainfall and population.
