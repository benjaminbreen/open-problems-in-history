Did the Black Death reach sub-Saharan Africa in the fourteenth century?

Plague devastated Egypt and North Africa in 1347–49, but whether it crossed the Sahara or reached the Horn and East Africa is unresolved.

Disease · Africa · 1340–1500science

Could use help from geneticists, archaeologists and epidemiologists.

Why it matters

If plague reached West, East or Northeast Africa, it would offer an explanation for site abandonments, settlement shifts and political reorganizations that archaeologists date to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, from the forest zone of southern Ghana to Ife, and it would change how historians read the demographic and political background to the Atlantic encounter. It also bears on the global history of the second pandemic, on whether the Yersinia pestis lineages now endemic in Central and East Africa descend from medieval introductions, and on the long-standing assumption that Africa south of the Sahara was untouched while Eurasia and North Africa lost a large share of their populations.

Why it is open

Written sources from sub-Saharan Africa for the fourteenth century are few, and Arabic chroniclers who described plague in Egypt and the Maghrib were largely silent about lands beyond the Sahara, a silence that can be read either as absence of plague or absence of reporting. Archaeological signs such as abandonments, mass burials or population decline have other possible causes (drought, war, trade shifts), and few sites are dated precisely enough to tie them to the 1340s–1400s. No medieval Y. pestis genome has yet been published from sub-Saharan Africa, so the genetic argument rests on inferences from the phylogeny of modern African strains.

What would count as a solution

Recovery of Yersinia pestis DNA, radiocarbon-dated to c. 1340–1500, from human remains at one or more sub-Saharan sites, placed on the second-pandemic phylogeny; or, conversely, systematic negative screening of well-dated fourteenth- and fifteenth-century burials across several regions combined with a full survey of the textual evidence showing no plague references.

Potential approach

  • Start from the regional work already done: (2018) on the fourteenth-century abandonment of earthwork settlements in southern Ghana and at Ife, (2018) on which archaeological signatures can and cannot distinguish plague from drought or war, (2018) on Ge'ez lexical and talismanic evidence from the Horn, and (2018) on what the phylogeny of modern African strains does and does not show. (2022) and (2024) set out what the already excavated skeletal material from Benin City and Ile-Ife can bear, including a mass grave opened in the early 1960s.
  • Build from the archaeological literature a geo-referenced register of candidate sites and of human remains, with radiocarbon determinations recorded as calibrated ranges and with the original laboratory codes. The choice that shapes everything after it is whether a candidate is defined by settlement disruption or by dated human remains whose range overlaps c. 1340-1500; these are different classes of evidence and should be kept separate rather than merged into one map of 'plague sites'.
  • In parallel, compile a dated register of references to epidemic mortality, and of the places and years for which sources are silent, from Arabic and Ge'ez texts, fifteenth-century Portuguese accounts in the , and the West African manuscripts imaged from and the Ethiopian collections in the . Most of these manuscripts are later copies, so each reference needs its witness dated by specialists before it counts as fourteenth-century testimony.
  • Some progress needs no new excavation: re-screening the published shotgun data from African ancient-DNA studies held in the , including the medieval Swahili coast individuals sequenced by (2023), with authentication workflows such as the one described by (2023), and reporting coverage and detection limits for each individual. Even a set of negatives establishes how many dated burials have now been tested and how deeply.
  • The two hypotheses can be stated so that evidence separates them. Chouin (2018) and (2020) argue for fourteenth-century introductions south of the Sahara, while (2024), working from a large collection of modern isolates, dates the spread of the 1.ANT lineage into continental Africa to the late seventeenth century and after; a medieval genome from a sub-Saharan burial, placed on the second-pandemic framework of (2019), would decide between them, and its absence would not. Until then the honest output is region by region, plague attested, screened without detection, or untested, rather than a single continental verdict.
  • Everything beyond the computational re-screening depends on institutions: ancient-DNA laboratories, and museums and heritage authorities in Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania and Ethiopia, which hold both the remains and the excavation records, among them the and field archives of the . Sampling and radiocarbon dating should proceed under protocols agreed with those authorities and with descendant communities, and with African archaeologists and bioarchaeologists reviewing both the registers and any laboratory result.

geneticists
Re-screen published African ancient-DNA shotgun data in the European Nucleotide Archive for Yersinia pestis, reporting coverage and detection limits for each individual.
archaeologists
Build a geo-referenced register of candidate sites and human remains with calibrated radiocarbon ranges and laboratory codes, keeping settlement disruption and dated burials separate.
epidemiologists
State what mortality and settlement patterns plague would produce in the forest zone and the Horn, as distinct from drought or war, for testing against the site register.
