Which women wrote the unsigned stories in nineteenth-century newspapers?

A newspaper might print a novel one chapter at a time and drop the byline when it reprinted it. Following the chapters could restore a writer’s name and career.

Who printed the anonymous and false-imprint books of eighteenth-century Britain? →

Why it matters

Newspapers in different countries carried the same stories under different titles. A signed instalment in one town can supply the credit missing from an entire run elsewhere. Connecting several such runs could show a woman earning a living from fiction well beyond the readership of any book published under her name.

Why it is open

A pen name could belong to more than one writer. Editors shortened stories and syndicates distributed the same version to many papers. A resemblance in writing style cannot by itself supply an author’s identity or gender.

What would count as a solution

Link an unsigned serial to a named writer through independent evidence such as a copyright registration, a publisher’s payment or a signed version with a documented attribution. Measure mistaken identifications on known writers before proposing names for unknown ones.

Potential approach

  • Trace successive instalments in Chronicling America, Trove and Gallica through changes of title, byline and language. Count reprints of a common source together.
  • Fix the authorship test in advance using signed serials, including writers outside the candidate set. Test it on withheld works and report false matches by language and genre.
  • Follow promising matches into copyright ledgers, correspondence, syndicate notices and obituaries. Document the evidence that a pseudonym belonged to a particular person.
