What texts did Newton copy in the alchemical manuscripts whose sources are unidentified?

Most of Newton's million words on alchemy copy or digest named authors, but several recipe collections and a dialogue among his papers derive from exemplars no one has identified.

Texts & authorship · Europe · 1660–1700digitization

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers, archivists and librarians, computational linguists and conservation scientists.

Why it matters

Newton's alchemical papers are mostly his transcriptions, translations and digests of other writers, so what he copied, and from whom, is the main evidence for what he knew and when. The unattributed items are disproportionately practical (recipes, furnace notes, an adept's answers to a novice), and they bear directly on the dispute between readings of his alchemy as a religious and natural-philosophical search for an active spirit in matter ( 1975, 1991) and as a laboratory chymistry aimed at decoding the cover names of Starkey and others ( 2018). Identified exemplars would also date the copies, show which texts reached Newton in manuscript rather than print, and name the correspondents and collections (Boyle's circle, Starkey's papers, the who in 1675 communicated the text Newton copied as ) through which he obtained them.

Why it is open

Alchemical recipes repeat standard operations and materials across hundreds of authors, so a procedural resemblance rarely isolates one source, and many texts Newton used circulated only in manuscript, under pseudonyms or in translation. The comparison corpora are lopsided: English print is well transcribed in EEBO-TCP and a selection of Latin print in EMLAP, but later seventeenth-century Latin, German and Dutch chymistry and the large manuscript collections are mostly uncatalogued below the volume level and untranscribed. The records as 'clearly not Newton's own composition' with sources unidentified, as a transcript or translation of an anonymous unpublished work, and ff. 6r–8v as notes from an unidentified source; none has an agreed date.

What would count as a solution

For each manuscript (, , ff. 6r–8v), a named printed or manuscript exemplar, demonstrably older than Newton's copy, that shares distinctive readings, errors, omissions or ordering with it across several units, not merely the same operations. Where Newton combined sources, the test applies unit by unit, and a partial solution assigns each recipe or answer to a source or records a documented null search.

Potential approach

  • The editorial groundwork exists: the descriptions and the (1992) account of the 1936 Sotheby sale fix custody and contents, Figala, Harrison and Petzoldt (1992) reconstruct Newton's chymical library and his lists of writers, and the edition, described in et al. (2020) and (2025), encodes his citations so that identified sources can be separated from the residue.
  • Teams could align the Indiana of the three manuscripts, unit by unit, against EEBO-TCP for English print and EMLAP and Theatrum Chemicum for Latin, using matching that survives translation, abbreviation and symbol substitution. Distinctive anchors such as unusual quantities, apparatus, cover names, and the external folio numbers cited in are better search keys than common operations.
  • Precedents show what counts: (1987) showed that the 'Clavis', once read as Newton's own composition, is Starkey's text, and (2002) traced Starkey's manuscript processes through . Candidate matches should be judged the same way, by shared variants and errors, with philologists ruling on whether a resemblance indicates descent or common practice.
  • If no printed ancestor exists, the exemplar is likely a manuscript, and finding it needs digitization and item-level cataloguing of chymical manuscript collections: at the Bodleian, at the British Library, the at Yale and Boyle's papers at the Royal Society, with transcription models trained on seventeenth-century English, Latin and German hands.
  • Physical evidence can narrow dates independently: Indiana's description of records a watermark shared with other manuscripts, and systematic watermark and paper comparison across Newton's dated papers would test the catalogue's estimate of 1668–75 and the early to mid 1670s date given for Keynes MS 58.

Who can help

philologists and epigraphers
Collate Keynes MSS 31, 44 and 58 against candidate Latin, English, German and Dutch witnesses, recording shared errors, omissions and ordering rather than shared procedures.
archivists and librarians
Search catalogues of seventeenth-century chymical manuscripts in Ashmole, Sloane, Mellon and Boyle collections for recipe books matching the titles, sequences and folio numbering in Newton's copies.
computational linguists
Build alignment searches over the Newton TEI, EEBO-TCP and EMLAP that tolerate translation, alchemical symbols and spelling variation, and report ranked candidates with their null results.
conservation scientists
Compare watermarks, chain-line spacing and inks in Keynes MSS 31, 44 and 58 with dated Newton papers to narrow when each copy was made.

Existing work

  1. 1947
    John Maynard Keynes, Newton, the Man, in The Royal Society, Newton Tercentenary Celebrations. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press (lecture read in July 1946).Written by the purchaser of most of the alchemical papers at the 1936 sale, it called Newton 'the last of the magicians' and set the terms of later debate.
  2. 1975
    B. J. T. Dobbs, The Foundations of Newton's Alchemy, or 'The Hunting of the Greene Lyon', Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.First systematic study of the manuscripts, discussing Keynes MS 31 and MS 58 and identifying the 'Mr F.' of the Manna manuscript as Ezekiel Foxcroft.
  3. 1977
    Karin Figala, Newton as Alchemist, History of Science 15 (2): 102–137.Essay review of Dobbs that questioned several of its attributions and identifications, including that of 'Mr F.' as Foxcroft.
  4. 1980
    Richard S. Westfall, Never at Rest: A Biography of Isaac Newton, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Standard biography that integrated the alchemical papers into Newton's intellectual development and dated his alchemical reading and experiments.
  5. 1987
    William R. Newman, Newton's Clavis as Starkey's Key, Isis 78 (4): 564–574.Showed that the 'Clavis', which Dobbs had treated as Newton's own composition, is a text by George Starkey, the model case of a source identification changing interpretation.
  6. 1991
    B. J. T. Dobbs, The Janus Faces of Genius: The Role of Alchemy in Newton's Thought, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Argued that Newton's alchemy was a religiously motivated search for the divine agency acting in matter, closely bound to his theology and natural philosophy.
  7. 1992
    Karin Figala, John Harrison and Ulrich Petzoldt, De scriptoribus chemicis: Sources for the Establishment of Isaac Newton's (Al)chemical Library, in P. M. Harman and A. E. Shapiro (eds.), The Investigation of Difficult Things. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 135–179.Reconstructed Newton's chymical library from his own book lists, establishing which printed works he owned and could have copied.
  8. 1992
    Peter Spargo, Sotheby's, Keynes and Yahuda: the 1936 sale of Newton's manuscripts, in P. M. Harman and A. E. Shapiro (eds.), The Investigation of Difficult Things. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 115–134.Traced the dispersal of the Portsmouth papers lot by lot, the custodial history on which the Keynes manuscript catalogue rests.
  9. 2002
    William R. Newman and Lawrence M. Principe, Alchemy Tried in the Fire: Starkey, Boyle, and the Fate of Helmontian Chymistry, Chicago: University of Chicago Press.Reconstructed Starkey's laboratory practice from his notebooks and letters in Boyle's papers, the manuscript tradition behind much of what Newton copied.
  10. 2016
    William R. Newman, A preliminary reassessment of Newton's alchemy, in R. Iliffe and G. E. Smith (eds.), The Cambridge Companion to Newton, 2nd edn. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Reassessed Newton's alchemy in light of the digital edition and argued for its experimental, chymical character against a primarily spiritual reading.
  11. 2018
    William R. Newman, Newton the Alchemist: Science, Enigma, and the Quest for Nature's 'Secret Fire', Princeton: Princeton University Press.Comprehensive study built on the edition, arguing that Newton's alchemy was a laboratory chymistry aimed at decoding Starkey, Snyders and other authors.

    Jennifer M. Rampling, The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700, Chicago: University of Chicago Press.Traced how English alchemical texts, above all the Ripley corpus, were copied, glossed and reworked in manuscript, the culture in which Newton's exemplars circulated.
