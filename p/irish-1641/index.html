How many people were killed in the Irish rising of 1641?

Contemporary claims of 154,000 to 300,000 Protestant victims are rejected, but the modern figure of roughly 4,000 killed has never been built from a full victim-level count.

Demography · Europe · 1641–1642

Why it matters

The scale of settler deaths in the winter of 1641–42 shaped English and Scottish policy toward Ireland for a decade, was invoked to justify the Cromwellian conquest and the land confiscations of the 1650s, and remained a fixed point in Protestant and Catholic memory into the twentieth century. Whether the violence amounted to a general massacre, a set of local atrocities, or something in between bears on arguments about colonial violence, sectarian identity, and the comparative history of early modern mass killing. A defensible total, with its distribution in time and place, would also show how far the printed atrocity literature departed from the sworn testimony on which it drew.

Why it is open

The figures in circulation were produced for polemic: the Lords Justices relayed a claim of 154,000 dead in 1643, Sir John in 1646 wrote of over 300,000 British and Protestants murdered or expelled, and Sir William Petty later proposed about 37,000. Modern estimates, generally around 4,000 killed directly and perhaps 10,000–12,000 including deaths from exposure, hunger and disease, are largely concentrated on Ulster and rest on partial readings rather than a systematic count; Aidan argued in 2013 that even this consensus needs re-examination. The depositions are dominated by hearsay, the same killings are reported by many deponents with varying names and numbers, and Catholic victims of settler and army reprisals are poorly represented in a source collected from Protestants.

What would count as a solution

A published, deduplicated database of every death reported in the , each linked to the depositions that mention it and coded for eyewitness versus hearsay, place, date and manner, yielding a reproducible minimum count and a stated range for Protestant deaths, with Catholic deaths assessed from other sources.

Potential approach

  • The full transcription at and the twelve-volume edition make it feasible for AI agents to extract every reported killing, normalise personal and place names against seventeenth-century gazetteers and the Down Survey, and cluster reports of the same event across depositions.
  • Historians would set the rules for matching and for weighting hearsay, review uncertain clusters, and cross-check against , the and the 1650s High Court of Justice examinations, then add reprisal killings of Catholics from army and pamphlet sources to give a two-sided total.

Existing work

  1. 1646
    John Temple, The Irish Rebellion, London: R. White for Samuel Gellibrand.Founding Protestant narrative that drew on the depositions and claimed over 300,000 British and Protestants murdered, otherwise destroyed, or expelled by September 1643.
  2. 1884
    Mary Hickson, Ireland in the Seventeenth Century, or The Irish Massacres of 1641–2: Their Causes and Results, London: Longmans, Green (2 vols., preface by J. A. Froude).First substantial printed selection of depositions, framed as proof of a general massacre and the start of the modern controversy over their reliability.
  3. 1978
    M. Perceval-Maxwell, The Ulster Rising of 1641, and the Depositions, Irish Historical Studies 21 (82): 144–167.Source-critical assessment of the depositions as evidence for the Ulster violence and for the inflated contemporary death tolls.
  4. 1993
    Brian Mac Cuarta (ed.), Ulster 1641: Aspects of the Rising, Belfast: Institute of Irish Studies, Queen's University Belfast.Local studies of the rising, including Hilary Simms and Raymond Gillespie on the killings of winter 1641–42; reissued in 2020 with a bibliographical essay.
  5. 1994
    M. Perceval-Maxwell, The Outbreak of the Irish Rebellion of 1641, Dublin: Gill & Macmillan; Montreal: McGill-Queen's University Press.Standard political account of the origins and first months of the rising.
  6. 1997
    Ethan Howard Shagan, Constructing Discord: Ideology, Propaganda, and English Responses to the Irish Rebellion of 1641, Journal of British Studies 36 (1): 4–34.Shows how English print and parliamentary politics shaped and amplified reports of massacre.
  7. 2001
    Nicholas Canny, Making Ireland British, 1580–1650, Oxford: Oxford University Press.Uses the depositions extensively to reconstruct the plantation society and the dynamics of violence in 1641.
  8. 2012
    Eamon Darcy, Annaleigh Margey and Elaine Murphy (eds.), The 1641 Depositions and the Irish Rebellion, London: Pickering & Chatto.Essays from the 1641 Depositions Project, including corpus-linguistic work from the Aberdeen team.
  9. 2013
    Aidan Clarke, The '1641 Massacres', in Micheál Ó Siochrú and Jane Ohlmeyer (eds.), Ireland 1641: Contexts and Reactions (Manchester University Press), 37–51.Traces the history of death-toll estimates from Temple onward and argues that the consensus figure of about 4,000 itself rests on selective evidence and needs re-examination.
  10. 2013
    Eamon Darcy, The Irish Rebellion of 1641 and the Wars of the Three Kingdoms, Woodbridge: Royal Historical Society / Boydell Press.Examines how Dublin officials gathered and circulated deposition material and how far their claims were justified.
  11. 2013
    John Gibney, The Shadow of a Year: The 1641 Rebellion in Irish History and Memory, Madison: University of Wisconsin Press.History of the rival Protestant (general massacre) and Catholic (no massacre) traditions of interpreting 1641.
  12. 2014
    Aidan Clarke (principal ed.) et al., The 1641 Depositions, Dublin: Irish Manuscripts Commission, 12 vols., 2014–2020.Complete printed edition arranged by county, with indexes, complementing the online transcription.
  13. 2021
    John Walter, Crowds and Political Violence in Early Modern Ireland: Galway and the 1641 Depositions, Irish Historical Studies 45 (168): 178–202.Micro-historical reconstruction of two 1642 killings in Galway that shows how the depositions' framing distorts the character of popular violence.
  14. 2025
    Jane Ohlmeyer, Women and Sexual Violence in the "1641 Depositions", Law and History Review 43 (2): 221–238.Recent systematic reading of one category of violence across the depositions and of its later political use.
