How much of the cacao along Amazonian rivers grew from colonial-era plantings?

Cacao grew in Amazonia long before Europeans arrived. An eighteenth-century survey could help distinguish groves planted for colonial trade from the legacy of earlier Indigenous cultivation.

Economy · Americas · 1700–1800science

Could use help from GIS specialists and archaeologists.

Why it matters

Cacao trees in present-day forests help researchers reconstruct where people once managed the land. The same riverbank might have supported an Indigenous settlement, a mission and a commercial grove in succession. Knowing which plantings left a trace would change what those trees tell us about each period.

Why it is open

Francisco Xavier Ribeiro de Sampaio recorded settlements during his 1774–75 inspection of the Rio Negro. Colonial records also count cacao harvested or traded. A quantity of beans shipped from a village need not have been grown there, and a missing return says little about an unvisited tributary.

What would count as a solution

For the Rio Negro places that can be located with confidence, test whether documented planting predicts modern cacao abundance after accounting for river access, habitat and earlier settlement. Report the area the comparison covers and the uncertainty around any estimated colonial contribution.

Potential approach

  • Transcribe Sampaio’s returns and distinguish planted trees, harvested beans and cargo received from elsewhere. Match historical place names to rivers and settlements with an uncertainty range.
  • Compare forest plots near documented plantings with plots on comparable floodplains, including places with evidence of precolonial occupation.
  • Repeat the comparison after removing poorly located sites. Check the interpretation with local accounts of managed groves and later changes in land use.
