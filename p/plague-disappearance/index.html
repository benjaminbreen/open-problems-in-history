Why did plague disappear from western Europe after the early eighteenth century?

After nearly four centuries of recurring epidemics, plague retreated from western and central Europe between the 1660s and the 1720s while persisting in the Ottoman Mediterranean, and no explanation commands agreement.

Disease · Europe · 1650–1850sciencedigitization

Why it matters

The retreat of plague is one of the earliest large declines in European mortality, and its cause determines whether early modern states could control epidemic disease through quarantine, cordons and health passes, or whether the decline was ecological or biological and owed little to policy. The answer bears on demographic models of the eighteenth-century population rise, on the divergence between western Europe and the Ottoman and Russian lands where plague continued into the nineteenth century, and on current arguments about the efficacy of border controls against epidemics.

Why it is open

The retreat was gradual and uneven: London's last major epidemic was 1665, central Europe and the Baltic suffered in 1708–13, Provence in 1720–22, Messina in 1743, Moscow in 1771, and Noja (1815–16) and Mallorca (1820) saw late seaborne outbreaks, so any explanation must account for a moving frontier rather than a single date. Each main hypothesis (quarantine, rat or flea ecology, host immunity, climate, pathogen change) predicts the same broad outcome and is hard to falsify from mortality data alone. The genetic evidence has opened a parallel dispute over whether European plague was sustained by local reservoirs or repeatedly reintroduced from Asia, and ancient DNA samples remain few for the last outbreaks.

What would count as a solution

A spatially explicit model, fitted to dated outbreak records and to the records of health offices on ship arrivals, quarantines and cordons, that reproduces the sequence and geography of the last western European outbreaks and correctly predicts which ports and regions escaped introduction when exposed, with its assumptions about reservoirs consistent with the ancient DNA phylogeny.

Potential approach

  • Build a georeferenced, dated catalogue of plague outbreaks from 1650 to 1850 across Europe and the Mediterranean, starting from 's inventory and extending it with regional studies, bills of mortality and parish registers, with source citations for every entry.
  • Extract arrivals, origins, bills of health, quarantine durations and lazaretto incidents from the , Venice, Livorno, Genoa, Dubrovnik and Malta, which together record many near-misses where infected ships were stopped; much of this requires new digitization, building on the online .
  • Link these to Levant shipping and consular reports of plague in Ottoman ports to estimate exposure for each European port by year, and test whether quarantine stringency, rather than exposure, explains which ports escaped.
  • Work with palaeogeneticists to sample burials from the last outbreaks (Provence, the Baltic, Messina, southern Italy, the Balkans) and with ecologists on rodent and flea faunal remains, so that reservoir and reintroduction scenarios can be tested against the documentary model.

Existing work

  1. 1975
    Jean-Noël Biraben, Les hommes et la peste en France et dans les pays européens et méditerranéens, 2 vols. Paris and The Hague: Mouton, 1975–1976.Standard inventory of plague outbreaks in France and the Mediterranean to 1850, which credited sanitary cordons and maritime quarantine with the retreat.
  2. 1980
    Andrew B. Appleby, The Disappearance of Plague: A Continuing Puzzle, Economic History Review 33 (2).Rejected the brown rat replacement theory on chronological grounds, doubted the efficacy of quarantine, and suggested instead that black rat populations acquired resistance.
  3. 1981
    Paul Slack, The Disappearance of Plague: An Alternative View, Economic History Review 34 (3).Reply to Appleby arguing that human intervention, chiefly maritime quarantine and land cordons against reintroduction, was the decisive factor.
  4. 1986
    Daniel Panzac, Quarantaines et lazarets: l'Europe et la peste d'Orient (XVIIe–XXe siècles), Aix-en-Provence: Édisud.History of the Mediterranean quarantine system that, with his work on plague in the Ottoman Empire, framed Europe's escape as protection against continuing Ottoman epidemics.
  5. 2000
    Edward A. Eckert, The Retreat of Plague from Central Europe, 1640–1720: A Geomedical Approach, Bulletin of the History of Medicine 74 (1): 1–28.Mapped the shrinking geography of outbreaks in central Europe and argued that plague contracted toward persistent regional foci rather than vanishing uniformly.
  6. 2013
    Guido Alfani, Plague in seventeenth-century Europe and the decline of Italy: an epidemiological hypothesis, European Review of Economic History 17 (4): 408–430.Showed that the last seventeenth-century plagues struck southern Europe far harder than the north, a contrast that any account of the retreat must explain.
  7. 2015
    Boris V. Schmid, Ulf Büntgen, W. Ryan Easterday, Christian Ginzler, Lars Walløe, Barbara Bramanti and Nils Chr. Stenseth, Climate-driven introduction of the Black Death and successive plague reintroductions into Europe, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 112 (10): 3020–3025.Linked European port outbreaks to tree-ring climate signals in Central Asia with a lag, arguing for repeated reintroductions from Asian rodent reservoirs rather than European foci.
  8. 2015
    Nükhet Varlık, Plague and Empire in the Early Modern Mediterranean World: The Ottoman Experience, 1347–1600, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Reconstructed Ottoman plague chronology and argued for endemic foci within the empire, the source region against which western quarantine was directed.
  9. 2016
    Kirsten I. Bos, Alexander Herbig, Jason Sahl, Nicholas Waglechner, Mathieu Fourment, Stephen A. Forrest et al., Eighteenth century Yersinia pestis genomes reveal the long-term persistence of an historical plague focus, eLife 5: e12994.Genomes from Marseille victims of 1722 descended from the Black Death lineage and matched no living focus, suggesting a now-extinct reservoir in or near Europe that persisted for centuries.
  10. 2018
    Katharine R. Dean, Fabienne Krauer, Lars Walløe, Ole Christian Lingjærde, Barbara Bramanti, Nils Chr. Stenseth and Boris V. Schmid, Human ectoparasites and the spread of plague in Europe during the Second Pandemic, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115 (6): 1304–1309.Mortality models for nine outbreaks fit human flea and louse transmission better than rat-borne transmission, which bears on whether rat ecology could explain the retreat.
  11. 2019
    Maria A. Spyrou, Marcel Keller, Rezeda I. Tukhbatova, Christiana L. Scheib, Elizabeth A. Nelson, Aida Andrades Valtueña et al., Phylogeography of the second plague pandemic revealed through analysis of historical Yersinia pestis genomes, Nature Communications 10: 4470.Thirty-four genomes showed post-Black Death diversification into distinct clades that may have formed more than one reservoir in or near Europe, and a genomic deletion in late-pandemic strains.
  12. 2020
    Meriam Guellil, Oliver Kersten, Amine Namouchi, Stefania Luciani, Isolina Marota, Caroline A. Arcini et al., A genomic and historical synthesis of plague in 18th century Eurasia, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 117 (45): 28328–28335.Eighteenth-century genomes, including the first from the Caucasus, used to argue for introductions from reservoirs outside Europe during the pandemic's last phase.
  13. 2020
    Alex Chase-Levenson, The Yellow Flag: Quarantine and the British Mediterranean World, 1780–1860, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Study of lazarettos and quarantine practice in Malta and the Mediterranean that assesses what the system did and did not achieve against plague.
  14. 2021
    Paul Slack, Perceptions of plague in eighteenth-century Europe, Economic History Review 75 (1): 138–156.Compared the last great epidemics in Germany, France and Moscow, showing unanimous official reliance on rigid quarantine amid deep medical division over contagion, and set western Europe against Ottoman and ancient DNA evidence.
  15. 2022
    Nils Chr. Stenseth, Yuxin Tao, Chutian Zhang, Barbara Bramanti, Ulf Büntgen et al., No evidence for persistent natural plague reservoirs in historical and modern Europe, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 119 (51): e2209816119.Argued from rodent and flea communities, soils and climate that western and central Europe could not sustain long-term wildlife reservoirs, supporting the reintroduction model against Bos et al.

    • Intendance sanitaire de Marseille (1640-1986)200 ERecords of the Marseille health board, including deliberations, bills of health (patentes de santé), quarantine and lazaretto registers, and the captains' declarations of arriving ships.
  • Archivio di Stato di Venezianot digitized Provveditori e sopraprovveditori alla sanità: deliberations, lazaretto records, bills of health, necrologi and reports from Venetian consuls in the LevantThe oldest and most continuous health magistracy archive in Europe, recording intelligence on plague in Ottoman and Adriatic ports and the quarantine of ships and goods.
    • Provveditori e Sopraprovveditori alla sanità (1486-1798)Fonds of the Venetian health magistracy, about 88 linear metres in 1,103 units; a catalogue record is given by the Western Sephardic Diaspora Roadmap project.
  • Državni arhiv u Dubrovniku (State Archives of Dubrovnik)not digitized Records of the Ragusan health office and the Ploče lazaretto, on the overland caravan and maritime routes from the Ottoman BalkansRagusa operated at the frontier with Ottoman plague and kept detailed health and quarantine records; access on site.
    • Uprava lazareta u Dubrovniku (Administration of the Dubrovnik lazarets)HR-DADU-107Summary inventory of the lazaret administration, 1814-1873 (36 boxes, 25 volumes), later than the early modern Ragusan health office but covering the nineteenth-century quarantine system.
  • Chambre de commerce et d'industrie Aix-Marseille-Provence, archives and heritage collectionspartly digitized Correspondence of French consuls in the Levant and Barbary and Levant trade recordsConsular reports of plague in Ottoman ports, the source used to build the eastern Mediterranean chronology against which European exposure can be measured.

  • Archives municipales de Marseillepartly digitized Municipal records of the 1720–22 epidemic, including deliberations of the échevins and burial and hospital recordsThe local record of the last major western European outbreak, needed to reconstruct its spread within the city and Provence.
