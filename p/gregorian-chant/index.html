Did Gregorian chant descend from Old Roman chant, or did both come from a common ancestor?

Frankish 'Gregorian' and city-of-Rome 'Old Roman' chant share texts and melodic outlines but differ in detail, and Roman melodies were written down only from 1071.

Texts & authorship · Europe · 680–1250

Could use help from musicologists, statisticians and data scientists and philologists and epigraphers.

Why it matters

Gregorian chant is the foundation of Western music writing, modal theory and the later polyphonic tradition, and from the ninth century it was presented as the authentic chant of Rome. Whether it records the Roman repertory imported under Pippin III and Charlemagne, a Frankish re-composition of it, or a fusion with Gallican chant decides what the Carolingian 'romanization' of liturgy actually involved. Because the Roman melodies were written down roughly three centuries after the transfer, the answer is also a test case for how faithfully unnotated melody can be transmitted, which bears on oral-tradition arguments well beyond chant.

Why it is open

The decisive period is undocumented in pitch. The Frankish chant books of about 800 have texts only; the neumed graduals of about 900 to 970, such as , and , record melodic shape but not exact pitch; and Roman melodies survive only from (1071) and a handful of later books. The same differences can be explained in opposite directions: the Franks may have simplified an ornate Roman style, the Romans may have drifted into formulaic uniformity during three centuries of oral transmission, or the Romans may have reworked imported Gregorian melodies in their own idiom. Comparison has proceeded genre by genre and chant by chant with qualitative judgement, and most Old Roman melodies are still not encoded in machine-readable form.

What would count as a solution

A pre-registered comparison of rival transmission models (Old Roman ancestral to Gregorian, independent descent from a common eighth-century Roman tradition, and Roman reworking of imported Gregorian chant), fitted to aligned melodies of all shared Mass propers and scored by how well each predicts held-out readings in early pitched and neumed sources, with decision margins fixed before the test. A model that wins across genres by the agreed margin settles the question for practical purposes; a result that differs by genre is also an answer, showing that layers of the repertory have different histories.

Potential approach

  • The comparative literature is the starting point: (1956) framed the 'central problem', (1970) edited , (1974) and (1980) recast both repertories as products of oral transmission, and genre studies by (1998) on offertories, (2002) on eighth-mode tracts and (2010) on offertories supply chant-by-chant judgements about which readings look older.
  • The rival positions should be stated as testable predictions before any data are fitted: Roman priority in a Frankish adaptation, as in Hucke (1980) and (2000); Old Roman as a later, formulaic development of the common tradition, as in (2002) and Maloy (2010); and (2003), in which the Franks drew on Gallican material and the Romans later absorbed and remodelled Gregorian melodies. Each predicts a different direction of change for ornament, cadence formulas and melodic range.
  • Teams could encode every Old Roman Mass proper in , Vat. lat. 5319 and (and Office chants in and ) in volpiano, link them to Gregorian melodies already in the through Cantus Index identifiers, and add the neume contours of , and as partial observations of pitch direction.
  • The phylogenetic methods of Hajič, Lanz and (2025) and the ancestral melody reconstruction of Ballen, Mühlová and Hajič (2026), which so far cover Gregorian sources only, can be extended to the two repertories, with care taken that formulaic convergence (shared stock cadences) is not mistaken for shared descent; (1997) is an earlier computer-assisted study of Old Roman formulas to build on.
  • Held-out testing gives the decision: hide a set of early pitched witnesses and the neumed contours of the earliest Frankish graduals, and score each model on how well it predicts them, with thresholds, genres and alignment rules published in advance so that the result cannot be tuned after the fact.
  • Text evidence can serve as an independent check: the psalter forms and biblical variants in shared chant texts, which Pfisterer (2002) used, date the wording of each repertory without depending on the melodies.

Who can help

musicologists
Encode the Old Roman Mass propers of Bodmer 74, Vat. lat. 5319 and San Pietro F 22 in volpiano, aligned chant by chant with Gregorian sources through Cantus Index identifiers.
statisticians and data scientists
Pre-register rival transmission models (Roman-derived, common ancestor, later Roman reworking of Gregorian) and score each by how well it predicts held-out readings in early pitched sources.
philologists and epigraphers
Classify the psalter and biblical text variants in shared chant texts as Roman, Gallican or Vulgate forms, as independent evidence for which repertory kept the older wording.

Existing work

  1. 1956
    Willi Apel, The Central Problem of Gregorian Chant, Journal of the American Musicological Society 9 (2): 118–127.Named the coexistence of the Gregorian and Old Roman repertories the central problem of chant history and argued that the Roman melodies were the later and less authentic tradition.
  2. 1970
    Bruno Stäblein and Margareta Landwehr-Melnicki, Die Gesänge des altrömischen Graduale Vat. lat. 5319, Monumenta Monodica Medii Aevi 2. Kassel: Bärenreiter.Transcription of an Old Roman gradual with Stäblein's introduction, which characterised the Roman style and proposed that both repertories originated in Rome.
  3. 1974
    Leo Treitler, Homer and Gregory: The Transmission of Epic Poetry and Plainchant, The Musical Quarterly 60 (3): 333–372.Applied oral-formulaic theory to chant, arguing that melodies were reconstructed in performance from formulas and that the Roman and Frankish versions are separate realisations of a shared oral tradition.
  4. 1980
    Helmut Hucke, Toward a New Historical View of Gregorian Chant, Journal of the American Musicological Society 33 (3): 437–467.Rejected the legend of Gregory the Great's authorship and argued that Gregorian chant took shape when the Franks received and fixed Roman chant transmitted orally in the eighth century.
  5. 1987
    Kenneth Levy, Charlemagne's Archetype of Gregorian Chant, Journal of the American Musicological Society 40 (1): 1–30.Argued from the agreement of the earliest neumed sources that a notated Gregorian archetype existed by about 800, which would make the Frankish melodies fixed far earlier than the Roman ones.
  6. 1997
    Max Haas, Mündliche Überlieferung und altrömischer Choral: Historische und analytische computergestützte Untersuchungen, Bern: Peter Lang.Computer-assisted analysis of the formulaic construction of Old Roman melodies, treating them as the product of oral transmission.
  7. 1998
    Joseph Dyer, Tropis semper variantibus: Compositional Strategies in the Offertories of Old Roman Chant, Early Music History 17: 1–60.Analysed how Old Roman offertories vary and recombine stock melodic material, setting the Roman style against its Gregorian counterparts.
  8. 2000
    James McKinnon, The Advent Project: The Later-Seventh-Century Creation of the Roman Mass Proper, Berkeley: University of California Press.Argued that the Roman schola cantorum created the annual cycle of Mass propers as a planned project in the late seventh century, giving both repertories a datable Roman starting point.
  9. 2002
    Andreas Pfisterer, Cantilena Romana: Untersuchungen zur Überlieferung des gregorianischen Chorals, Beiträge zur Geschichte der Kirchenmusik 11. Paderborn: Schöningh.Argued from patterns of melodic variants for a long, stable oral phase in the Frankish tradition and concluded that Old Roman chant is not archaic but a later, decadent form of the common tradition.
  10. 2002
    Emma Hornby, Gregorian and Old Roman Eighth-Mode Tracts: A Case Study in the Transmission of Western Chant, Aldershot: Ashgate (reissued Routledge, 2019).Compared the two traditions' eighth-mode tracts phrase by phrase, treating them as two expressions of a single shared melodic system, each altered in transmission.
  11. 2003
    Kenneth Levy, Gregorian Chant and the Romans, Journal of the American Musicological Society 56 (1): 5–41.Proposed that the Franks rejected improvisatory Roman chant in favour of Gallican melodies, and that Gregorian chant later reached Rome, where singers remodelled parts of it in Roman style.
  12. 2010
    Rebecca Maloy, Inside the Offertory: Aspects of Chronology and Transmission, New York: Oxford University Press.Edition and study of the offertories arguing that three centuries of oral transmission made the Old Roman versions more formulaic, so they do not simply preserve the eighth-century state of the melodies.
  13. 2018
    Susan Rankin, Writing Sounds in Carolingian Europe: The Invention of Musical Notation, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.Reconstructs how and when neume notation emerged in Carolingian centres, the evidence against which claims of an early notated Gregorian archetype must be judged.
  14. 2025
    Jan Hajič jr, Vojtěch Lanz and Gustavo A. Ballen, Genome of melody: applying bioinformatics to study the evolution of Gregorian chant, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 380 (1940).Showed that phylogenetic models fitted to aligned chant melodies recover historically plausible patterns of melodic change among Gregorian sources, with some institutional networks mattering more than geography.
  15. 2026
    Gustavo A. Ballen, Klára Hedvika Mühlová and Jan Hajič jr, What did the dove sing to Pope Gregory? Ancestral melody reconstruction in Gregorian chant using Bayesian phylogenetics, PLOS ONE 21 (5): e0350014.Reconstructed ancestral melodies on a time-calibrated tree of fourteen Gregorian sources, and named the encoding of Old Roman sources as the next step for testing their relation to Gregorian chant.
