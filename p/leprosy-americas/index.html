Do early American texts preserve memories of leprosy before European contact?

Ancient DNA shows that one cause of leprosy was present in the Americas thousands of years ago. Early written accounts might tell us how people recognized and understood the illness.

Disease · Americas · 1492–1700

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers and epidemiologists.

Why it matters

In 2025 two research teams identified Mycobacterium lepromatosis, one of the bacteria that cause Hansen’s disease, in ancient American remains. A description in an Indigenous language could preserve a name, a remembered case or the distinctions a healer made between illnesses that Europeans grouped together.

Why it is open

Colonial writers used words such as lepra for several skin conditions. A missionary might supply a European label for a local word or copy an earlier book. A description alone rarely separates the two bacteria that cause Hansen’s disease, and the date of a manuscript may be much later than the events it recounts.

What would count as a solution

Publish dated, independently translated passages that specify who observed the illness and when. Identify which details fit Hansen’s disease, which fit alternatives such as treponemal disease, and whether the witness actually places the condition before local contact.

Potential approach

  • Start with the Nahuatl and Spanish columns of the Florentine Codex, then compare colonial dictionaries, the Relaciones Geográficas, the Jesuit Relations and early medical books.
  • Keep patients’ descriptions, healers’ terms and translators’ glosses distinct. Check the page image for every passage quoted.
  • Have language specialists and historians of medicine review the readings independently, including plausible alternatives to leprosy.
