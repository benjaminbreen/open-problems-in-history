Which old books preserve overlooked word lists from little-documented languages?

A traveller might print a page of words in a book about something else. For a language with few surviving records, that page could preserve words found nowhere else.

Languages & scripts · Global · 1550–1900

Could use help from computational linguists and philologists and epigraphers.

Why it matters

Some early accounts name the person who supplied the words. Others identify only a port, or give a language the name of a neighbouring people. Recovering the page and its history can give speakers, descendants and linguists access to a record that library catalogues describe only as a voyage or a natural history.

Why it is open

A pilot catalogue, Wordes of their Language, includes 44 tables covering 42 languages. It also documents a trap for counting discoveries. Thomas Herbert’s 1634 Malay vocabulary repeats material printed by Jacob van Neck’s expedition in 1601. Two printed witnesses can lead back to one encounter.

What would count as a solution

For a defined set of books, publish each overlooked list next to the scan, with its wording, date, likely language and earlier printings. Check earlier bibliographies and state how many distinct records the search added.

Potential approach

  • Test the search on known tables in Early English Books Online, then extend it to Eighteenth Century Collections Online, HathiTrust, Internet Archive, the , Gallica and Delpher.
  • Compare the entries with ASJP and Lexibank word lists, using shuffled meanings to measure accidental matches. Check language names and alternative identifications against .
  • Ask specialists and community readers to examine uncertain readings, names and attributions. Trace identical errors across editions to identify lists copied from earlier books.
