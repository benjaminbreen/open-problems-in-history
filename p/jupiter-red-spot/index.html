Is Jupiter’s Great Red Spot the same storm astronomers saw in the seventeenth century?

Jupiter has a storm large enough to see from Earth. Drawings in old observing books could tell us whether it survived for centuries or replaced an earlier storm.

Why it matters

Cassini and other observers watched a dark patch on Jupiter that they called the Permanent Spot. Its accepted record ends in 1713. A drawing from 1831 is usually taken as the first sighting of today’s Great Red Spot. Whether they saw one storm or two changes how long we know such storms can last.

Why it is open

and colleagues argued in 2024 that the two spots were distinct. In Paris register D3/28, a dark knot appears at the end of a southern cloud band on three nights in 1714. Its motion is consistent across the drawings. A long-lived bend in the band could also account for it. Marseille timings from 1773–74 show how easily two markings can be mistaken for one.

What would count as a solution

A sequence of dated drawings or timed crossings would need to track a feature of compatible shape, latitude and motion between observing seasons. Years in which observers repeatedly saw the right face of Jupiter and drew smaller features could also put a limit on survival.

Potential approach

  • Start with the 1713 reference drawing in Paris and the three 1714 sightings. Use recorded eclipses of Jupiter’s moons to check clock times and drawing orientation.
  • Compare the Marseille books with independent and Greenwich observations and notes in printed astronomical journals. Convert local times to the longitude facing Earth.
  • Mark separately the nights with a suitable view, nights with a moon’s shadow, and seasons with no useful drawings.
