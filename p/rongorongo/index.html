Was rongorongo an independent invention of writing?

The undeciphered glyphs carved on about two dozen wooden objects from Rapa Nui may be a pre-contact invention of writing or a nineteenth-century response to European literacy.

Languages & scripts · Pacific · 1400–1870sciencedecipherment

Could use help from computational linguists, archaeologists and conservation scientists.

Why it matters

Writing is generally held to have been invented independently only a handful of times (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, Mesoamerica), so a pre-contact Rapa Nui script would add a case from a small, isolated island society without cities or bureaucratic states. That would bear on theories that tie writing to administration and urbanism and on what Polynesian societies could develop in isolation. If instead the script arose after the Spanish annexation ceremony of 1770, when islanders were asked to mark a document, it would be a well-documented case of stimulus diffusion, and the question of whether the glyphs encode language at all would remain separate.

Why it is open

The corpus is small (fewer than 30 objects, about 15,000 glyphs in standard counts), the script ceased to be used in the 1860s after slave raids and epidemics, and nineteenth-century attempts to elicit readings from islanders produced unreliable results. Radiocarbon dates on the wood give only a terminus post quem for carving: most dated objects fall in the late eighteenth or nineteenth century, and the one fifteenth-century result (Tablet D, ) comes from non-native wood that could have been carved long after it was felled or arrived. Whether the glyphs record speech is itself disputed; claims of logosyllabic structure (Davletshin) and of a lunar calendar on () have not been converted into an agreed decipherment, and statistical tests of 'language-likeness' have been shown to misclassify non-linguistic symbol systems.

What would count as a solution

Direct dates on residues or carving-associated material, or on wood from additional inscribed objects, that securely place glyph carving before 1722, combined with a reading of sign sequences that predicts Rapa Nui words or parallel passages in unseen text and outperforms matched non-linguistic controls.

Potential approach

  • Compile a single, versioned digital corpus from 's transcription and the newer 3D models and transcriptions ( et al.; Horley), with explicit sign-list choices recorded so that results can be rerun under alternative segmentations.
  • Use research agents to run statistical comparisons of rongorongo sequences against both written languages and non-linguistic symbol systems (heraldry, genealogical tallies, Polynesian tattoo and rock-art motif sequences), following 's warnings about such tests, and report which conclusions survive changes in sign classification.
  • Test proposed readings (the lunar sequence, numeral and phonetic-complement hypotheses, parallel passages across tablets) by predicting held-out passages rather than by fitting known ones.
  • Extend radiocarbon and wood identification to the remaining tablets in , , , , and elsewhere, ideally on minimally invasive samples from carved surfaces or associated residues; this requires collaboration with the holding museums, wood anatomists and AMS radiocarbon laboratories.

computational linguists

archaeologists
Extend radiocarbon dating and wood identification to the undated tablets, using minimally invasive samples from carved surfaces or associated residues with AMS laboratories.
conservation scientists
Work with the holding museums in Washington, St Petersburg, Santiago, London and Berlin to identify sampling points on the tablets that minimize damage to carved surfaces.

Existing work

  1. 1958
    Thomas S. Barthel, Grundlagen zur Entzifferung der Osterinselschrift, Hamburg: Cram, de Gruyter.Established the standard sign catalogue, the letter designations of the texts and the first systematic study of parallel passages.
  2. 1990
    Jacques B. M. Guy, The lunar calendar of Tablet Mamari, Journal de la Société des Océanistes 91 (2): 135–149.Argued that a passage on Tablet C records a lunar calendar with intercalation, the most widely accepted identification of content in the corpus.
  3. 1996
    Konstantin Pozdniakov, Les bases du déchiffrement de l'écriture de l'île de Pâques, Journal de la Société des Océanistes 103 (2): 289–303.Reduced Barthel's inventory to a smaller set of basic signs using distributional analysis, arguing for a syllabic core.
  4. 1997
    Steven Roger Fischer, Rongorongo: The Easter Island Script. History, Traditions, Texts, Oxford: Clarendon Press.Comprehensive corpus study that argued the script was inspired by the 1770 Spanish annexation document, while also proposing a disputed procreation-chant reading.
  5. 2005
    Catherine Orliac, The Rongorongo tablets from Easter Island: botanical identification and 14C dating, Archaeology in Oceania 40 (3): 115–119.Identified tablet woods, including Thespesia populnea and imported species, and published a radiocarbon date that placed one tablet in the late eighteenth or nineteenth century.
  6. 2009
    Tomi S. Melka, Some Considerations about the Kohau Rongorongo Script in the Light of a Statistical Analysis of the 'Santiago Staff', Cryptologia 33 (1): 24–73.Statistical analysis of the longest text, discussing whether its repetitive structure fits a list, a chant or running language.
  7. 2009
    Paul Horley, Rongorongo Script: Carving Techniques and Scribal Corrections, Journal de la Société des Océanistes 129: 249–261.Showed evidence of planned layout and scribal corrections, consistent with a trained writing practice.
  8. 2012
    Albert Davletshin, Numerals and phonetic complements in the Kohau rongorongo script of Easter Island, Journal of the Polynesian Society 121 (3): 243–274.Proposed numerals and phonetic complements as evidence that the script records Rapa Nui language.
  9. 2014
    Richard Sproat, A Statistical Comparison of Written Language and Nonlinguistic Symbol Systems, Language 90 (2): 457–481.Showed that common statistical measures do not reliably separate writing from non-linguistic symbol systems, a constraint on any language-likeness test.
  10. 2021
    Rafał M. Wieczorek, Kamil E. Frankiewicz, Alexei A. Oskolski and Paul Horley, The rongorongo tablet from Berlin and the time-depth of Easter Island's writing system, Journal of Island and Coastal Archaeology 18 (2): 309–328.Identified the wood of Tablet O and dated it to the early nineteenth century.
  11. 2021
    Lorenzo Lastilla, Roberta Ravanelli, Miguel Valério and Silvia Ferrara, Modelling the Rongorongo tablets: A new transcription of the Échancrée tablet and the foundation for decipherment attempts, Digital Scholarship in the Humanities 37 (2): 497–516.Used high-resolution 3D models to produce a revised transcription of Tablet D.
  12. 2021
    Paul Horley, Rongorongo: Inscribed Objects from Rapa Nui, Viña del Mar: Rapanui Press.Corpus with editions and photographs of all known rongorongo inscribed objects.
  13. 2022
    Albert Davletshin, The script of Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is logosyllabic, the language is East Polynesian: evidence from cross-readings, Journal of the Polynesian Society 131 (2): 185–220.Argued from cross-readings between parallel passages that the script is logosyllabic and records an East Polynesian language.
  14. 2024
    Silvia Ferrara, Laura Tassoni, Bernd Kromer, Lukas Wacker, Michael Friedrich, Francesca Tonini, Lorenzo Lastilla, Roberta Ravanelli and Sahra Talamo, The invention of writing on Rapa Nui (Easter Island). New radiocarbon dates on the Rongorongo script, Scientific Reports 14: 2794.Dated four tablets in Rome; three fell in the late seventeenth to nineteenth century, while the non-native wood of Tablet D yielded a fifteenth-century felling date, which the authors took as suggesting pre-contact use.
