How much American silver reached China between 1550 and 1800?

Estimates of the share of Spanish American silver that ended up in China vary widely because mint, shipping and Chinese monetary records have never been reconciled in one account.

Economy · Global · 1550–1800

Could use help from economists, archivists and librarians and statisticians and data scientists.

Why it matters

The volume of silver entering China is central to the argument that world trade became global after the founding of Manila in 1571, to explanations of the Ming fiscal shift to silver, and to debates over whether a contraction of silver imports helped bring down the Ming in the 1630s–40s. It also bears on European price history, on the Dutch and English East India Companies' balance of trade, and on nineteenth-century arguments that the reversal of silver flows destabilised the Qing.

Why it is open

Silver reached China by several routes (the Manila galleons, the Cape route via European companies, overland through Central Asia, and from Japan, which was a larger supplier than the Americas for parts of the period), and each route is documented in different archives with different biases. Registered mint and treasury output understates production because of contraband and unregistered silver, and smuggling on the Acapulco–Manila route was extensive. Chinese sources record taxes, prices and exchange rates rather than imports, so arrivals must be inferred, and published estimates (, Flynn and Giráldez, von Glahn, ) rest on different assumptions about leakage, re-export and Japanese supply.

What would count as a solution

A route-by-route, decade-by-decade mass balance for 1550–1800 in which American registered production plus an explicit contraband estimate equals the sum of silver retained in the Americas, shipped to Europe, re-exported to Asia, and carried across the Pacific, with China's share derived from these flows and checked against Japanese exports and Chinese monetary indicators (silver–copper exchange rates, tax receipts in silver, price levels).

Potential approach

  • The registered production series of (1988) and (2010), the global accounting of (1990) and the import estimates of (1982), (1995) and (1996) are the base. (1965) fixed the scale of Japanese supply, and the galleon histories of (2014) and (2015) document how much of the Pacific trade escaped registration.
  • Assemble every published figure into one linked dataset, recording route, decade, original unit (pesos, marks or taels), fineness and whether the number is registered or estimated. Converting all of it to tonnes of fine silver with documented assumptions is what makes the routes comparable and exposes double counting where the same silver appears in mint, treasury and shipping series.
  • For the Pacific route, read the and in the , located through (1989), against the in the described by (1980). A reconciled Acapulco–Manila series for 1571–1815, set beside contemporary complaints about smuggling, would be useful on its own.
  • Rival readings of the seventeenth century can then be tested directly. Atwell's case for a collapse of imports after the 1630s and von Glahn's reply in the Journal of Economic History predict different decade totals, while (2008) and (2002) disagree on how large the inflow was relative to the Chinese economy; for Deshima and the of the East India Company give independent checks on the Japanese and Cape routes.
  • On the Chinese side, silver–copper exchange rates and provincial prices from the , and from 1736 the , offer a test of timing rather than volume. (2024), (2009) and (2006) frame the eighteenth-century and closing years, but contraband and the overland route through Central Asia mean China's share will remain a range under stated assumptions.
  • Progress depends on wider imaging of AGI and AGN legajos, on access to Qing memorials through the First Historical Archives, and on economic historians who read Spanish, Dutch and classical Chinese reviewing each series before it enters the totals.

Who can help

economists
Build a decade-by-decade mass balance for 1550 to 1800 that reconciles registered production, contraband estimates and route flows, and test it against Chinese silver-copper exchange rates and prices.
archivists and librarians
Read the Manila treasury accounts and galleon registers in the AGI against the Ramo Filipinas in the AGN to produce a reconciled Acapulco-Manila series for 1571 to 1815.
statisticians and data scientists
Convert every published figure to tonnes of fine silver with documented assumptions, then propagate the uncertainty to show where mint, treasury and shipping series double count.

    A. Kobata, The Production and Uses of Gold and Silver in Sixteenth- and Seventeenth-Century Japan, Economic History Review 18 (2): 245–266.Established the scale of Japanese silver exports, which competed with American silver as a source of China's supply.
  2. 1980
    María Cristina Urrutia de Stebelski, Guía del ramo Filipinas, México: Archivo General de la Nación (Serie Guías y Catálogos 5).Guide to the 63 volumes of the AGN's Ramo Filipinas, the Mexican side of the Manila galleon administration.
  3. 1982
    William S. Atwell, International Bullion Flows and the Chinese Economy circa 1530–1650, Past and Present 95: 68–90.Argued that Japanese and American silver transformed the late Ming economy and that a fall in imports after the 1630s contributed to the dynasty's collapse.
  4. 1988
    Richard L. Garner, Long-Term Silver Mining Trends in Spanish America: A Comparative Analysis of Peru and Mexico, American Historical Review 93 (4): 898–935.Long-run production series for Peruvian and Mexican silver from registered taxes, the base for most later flow estimates.
  5. 1989
    Antonia Heredia Herrera, Filipinas en el Archivo General de Indias, in El Extremo Oriente Ibérico: Investigaciones históricas, metodología y estado de la cuestión, Madrid: Agencia Española de Cooperación Internacional and CSIC, 149–183 (revised from Anuario de Estudios Americanos 37, 1980).Classification of the AGI's Audiencia de Filipinas fonds by series, with legajo ranges for the Manila treasury accounts and galleon registers.
  6. 1990
    Ward Barrett, World Bullion Flows, 1450–1800, in James D. Tracy (ed.), The Rise of Merchant Empires, Cambridge University Press, 224–254.First global accounting of bullion production and movement, estimating the shares of American silver that flowed to Europe and on to Asia.
  7. 1995
    Dennis O. Flynn and Arturo Giráldez, Born with a 'Silver Spoon': The Origin of World Trade in 1571, Journal of World History 6 (2): 201–221.Argued that Chinese demand, driven by silver's high value in China, created a global market linked through Manila from 1571 (link is the 2022 reprint).
  8. 1996
    Richard von Glahn, Fountain of Fortune: Money and Monetary Policy in China, 1000–1700, Berkeley: University of California Press.Monetary history of China that produced revised estimates of silver imports by source and period.
  9. 1996
    Richard von Glahn, Myth and Reality of China's Seventeenth-Century Monetary Crisis, Journal of Economic History 56 (2): 429–454.Challenged Atwell's thesis, arguing that silver imports did not collapse in the 1630s–40s on the scale required to explain the Ming fall.
  10. 2002
    Dennis O. Flynn and Arturo Giráldez, Cycles of Silver: Global Economic Unity through the Mid-Eighteenth Century, Journal of World History 13 (2): 391–427.Extended the argument into a two-cycle model of silver production and Chinese demand through the eighteenth century.
  11. 2006
    Man-houng Lin, China Upside Down: Currency, Society, and Ideologies, 1808–1856, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Asia Center (Harvard East Asian Monographs 270).Linked the end of the Spanish American silver supply during the independence wars to silver outflow and crisis in Qing China, framing the end point of the period.
  12. 2008
    Kent G. Deng, Miracle or Mirage? Foreign Silver, China's Economy and Globalization from the Sixteenth to the Nineteenth Centuries, Pacific Economic Review 13 (3): 320–357.Argued that foreign silver was small relative to the Chinese economy and questioned claims for its transformative effect.
  13. 2009
    Alejandra Irigoin, The End of a Silver Era: The Consequences of the Breakdown of the Spanish Peso Standard in China and the United States, 1780s–1850s, Journal of World History 20 (2): 207–244.Showed how Chinese demand for the Spanish peso as a unit, not only as bullion, shaped late flows and their reversal.
  14. 2010
    John J. TePaske (ed. Kendall W. Brown), A New World of Gold and Silver, Leiden: Brill (The Atlantic World 21).Posthumous compilation of decade-by-decade registered gold and silver output from Spanish and Portuguese American treasuries and mints, with discussion of contraband.
  15. 2014
    Mariano Bonialian, China en la América colonial: Bienes, mercados, comercio y cultura del consumo desde México hasta Buenos Aires, México: Instituto Mora; Buenos Aires: Biblos.Traced Asian goods and the silver paid for them through New Spain, Peru and the Río de la Plata, using unpublished Spanish, Mexican, Peruvian and Argentine records, much of it trade outside the registered galleon.
