How many people lived in China around 1600, and how many died between 1620 and 1680?

Late Ming population estimates run from about 150 million to over 200 million, so the scale of the mid-seventeenth-century collapse remains uncertain by tens of millions.

Demography · East Asia · 1580–1680

Could use help from demographers, statisticians and data scientists and GIS specialists.

Why it matters

The size of the late Ming population sets the denominator for comparisons of Chinese and European living standards, urbanization and per capita output, including recent historical national accounts. It also determines how catastrophic the Ming–Qing transition was: whether the wars, epidemics and famines of 1620–1680 killed a few tens of millions or a much larger share of the population, and how far the climate-crisis interpretation of the seventeenth century rests on China. Qing-era growth rates, and therefore arguments about the eighteenth-century population boom, depend on where the seventeenth-century trough lay.

Why it is open

Official Ming registers record roughly 60 million people throughout the dynasty, a figure that reflects fiscal quotas fixed after 1393 rather than headcounts, so every modern total is a reconstruction. Ping-ti (1959) proposed about 150 million for 1600; later work, including Cao Shuji's prefecture-level reconstruction, puts the late Ming peak close to 200 million, with some estimates higher. The central Yellow Register archive in Nanjing was lost, local gazetteer figures vary in what they count, and corrections for under-registration have been applied region by region with assumptions that are hard to compare.

What would count as a solution

A county- or prefecture-level population series for c.1580, 1630 and 1680 covering at least the core provinces, in which every adjustment to registered figures is stated as an explicit, reproducible rule, and whose implied densities and trends are shown to be consistent with independent evidence on cultivated land, land clearance, grain prices and tax arrears.

Potential approach

  • Agents can extract every household, ding and mouth figure, land total and tax quota from the digitized Ming and early Qing gazetteers into a single database keyed to county boundaries, recording the year and the registration category of each number.
  • Historians can then specify alternative under-registration models (for example, by ratio of ding to mouths, or by comparison with 1680s Qing registration) and run them consistently across counties, with the agents flagging outliers and internal contradictions for human review.
  • Cross-checks against grain-price series, reclamation memorials of the 1650s–1680s and local reports of depopulation would test whether a given model produces plausible regional trajectories.
  • The surviving Yellow Register fragments reused as book-backing paper could provide household-level calibration points if they are systematically imaged and transcribed.

Who can help

demographers
Specify alternative under-registration models, such as ding-to-mouth ratios or comparison with 1680s Qing registration, and apply each consistently across counties.
statisticians and data scientists
Flag outliers and internal contradictions in gazetteer household and land figures, and test each under-registration model against grain-price series and reclamation memorials.
GIS specialists
Key every gazetteer population, land and tax figure to CHGIS county boundaries by year, so densities and trends can be mapped across the core provinces.

Existing work

  1. 1959
    Ping-ti Ho, Studies on the Population of China, 1368–1953, Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press (Harvard East Asian Series 4).Founding study; drew on several thousand gazetteers to show that Ming registered figures were fiscal units and suggested a population of roughly 150 million by 1600.
  2. 1969
    Dwight H. Perkins, Agricultural Development in China, 1368–1968, Chicago: Aldine.Built long-run population and cultivated-acreage series from Ming–Qing tax records and gazetteers, arguing that output kept pace with a five- or six-fold population rise.
  3. 1980
    Liang Fangzhong 梁方仲, Zhongguo lidai hukou, tiandi, tianfu tongji 中国历代户口、田地、田赋统计, Shanghai: Shanghai renmin chubanshe.Standard compilation of dynastic household, land and land-tax statistics, including Ming provincial and prefectural series, on which most later reconstructions draw.
  4. 1998
    Martin Heijdra, The socio-economic development of rural China during the Ming, The Cambridge History of China, vol. 8: The Ming Dynasty, 1368–1644, Part 2.Reviewed the difficulties of Ming population and land registration figures and their implications for estimating rural growth over the dynasty.
  5. 2000
    Cao Shuji 曹树基, Zhongguo renkou shi, di si juan: Ming shiqi 中国人口史 第四卷 明时期, Shanghai: Fudan daxue chubanshe (Ge Jianxiong, ed., Zhongguo renkou shi).Prefecture-by-prefecture reconstruction of Ming population with region-specific corrections, placing the late Ming peak near 200 million around 1630.
  6. 2003
    Kent G. Deng, Fact or Fiction? Re-examination of Chinese Premodern Population Statistics, LSE Economic History Working Paper 76/03.Argued that modern estimates have drifted away from the official record, that the gap for post-1350 China can reach 200 million, and criticised the habit of linearising growth.

    Geoffrey Parker, Global Crisis: War, Climate Change and Catastrophe in the Seventeenth Century, New Haven: Yale University Press.Placed the Ming collapse and Manchu conquest (1618–84) at the centre of a climate-driven global crisis, relying on a large Chinese population loss.
