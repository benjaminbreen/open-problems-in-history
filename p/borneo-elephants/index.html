Are Borneo’s wild elephants native, or descendants of a royal gift?

Borneo’s elephants may descend from an ancient island herd or from animals shipped from Java. The answer could tell us whether Java’s lost elephants have living descendants.

Science & knowledge · Indian Ocean · 1300–1800science

Could use help from archivists and librarians and geneticists.

Why it matters

Genetic studies favour a long history of isolation. A competing explanation holds that elephants introduced from a now-vanished Javan population would bring an old genetic lineage with them. The age of a lineage and the date animals first lived wild on an island are two different questions.

Why it is open

A first search of Dutch East India Company records found elephants at Javanese courts, some imported from elsewhere. That leaves open both whether Java still had wild elephants and whether any court animals founded a herd in Borneo. A later retelling of a royal gift may compress several journeys into one.

What would count as a solution

A dated record identifying the animals’ origin, transfer through Sulu and release or breeding on Borneo would support the gift account. Secure evidence of wild elephants on Borneo before the proposed transfer would rule it out as the origin of the whole population.

Potential approach

  • Trace each version of the gift story to its earliest source. Check court accounts and Dutch correspondence for the origin, ownership and destination of individual animals.
  • Separate elephants kept at court from observations of herds in the wild. Test how often the same records mention other conspicuous forest animals.
  • Compare the earliest securely dated observations with archaeological finds and with the range of settlement dates the genetic evidence permits.
