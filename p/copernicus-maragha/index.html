How, if at all, did Copernicus learn of the Maragha-school planetary models?

Copernicus used devices found earlier in al-Ṭūsī and Ibn al-Shāṭir, but no document shows how a text containing them reached him or his teachers.

Science & knowledge · Europe · 1250–1543digitization

Could use help from archivists and librarians and philologists and epigraphers.

Why it matters

If Copernicus drew on the Ṭūsī couple and Ibn al-Shāṭir's lunar and Mercury models, the Copernican revolution sits at the end of a continuous Arabic and Persian reform of Ptolemaic astronomy rather than at a Latin new beginning. The answer bears on how historians describe the relation between Islamic science and the Renaissance, on the role of Byzantine and Jewish intermediaries in fifteenth-century Italy, and on Swerdlow's reconstruction of how Copernicus reached heliocentrism in the first place.

Why it is open

The mathematical parallels have been clear since (1959) and (1984), but Copernicus cites no Arabic author for these devices, and no Latin translation of al-Ṭūsī's Tadhkira or Ibn al-Shāṭir's Nihāyat al-sūl is known. The one securely located carrier, a Ṭūsī-couple diagram in a Byzantine Greek astronomical text in , proves the device reached Italy but not that Copernicus saw it. Skeptics ( 1995, 2014, 2018) argue that the devices could have been derived independently or from Latin sources such as Proclus, so parallels alone cannot settle the question.

What would count as a solution

A dated document (a manuscript, annotation, library inventory, loan record or letter) that places a text describing the Ṭūsī couple or Ibn al-Shāṭir's models in the hands of Copernicus, one of his teachers, or a scholar demonstrably in contact with him in Kraków, Bologna, Rome, Padua or Ferrara before about 1514. Alternatively, a documented exhaustive search of the relevant manuscript holdings that finds no such carrier would narrow the hypothesis to independent invention or oral transmission.

Potential approach

  • Start from the technical and textual work already done: the parallels established by (1959) and (1984), 's (2017) survey of the versions of the Ṭūsī couple in Arabic, Greek, Hebrew and Latin, (2019) on the Mercury models, and (2025) on intermediaries such as Moses Galeano. Set beside them the objections of (1995) and (2014), so that the documentary standard the skeptics ask for is stated before the search begins.
  • Build a census of every manuscript known to contain the Ṭūsī couple or an Ibn al-Shāṭir-type model, in any language, using and the catalogues as starting points. Each witness should be recorded by the specific device and variant it carries, since a rectilinear couple, a curvilinear one and a full lunar model point to different routes.
  • Cross-reference provenance, ownership marks, binding evidence and library inventories for these witnesses against a prosopography of Copernicus's teachers in Kraków and Bologna, his Paduan contemporaries such as Amico and Fracastoro, and the Greek and Jewish scholars in contact with them. Agents can draft the links, but specialists in palaeography and codicology should confirm each one.
  • Short of finding a carrier, a dated map of where each variant of the device is attested in Italy and Central Europe before 1514 would already narrow the question; it can start from and Bessarion's books in the . Later Paduan uses, such as the reciprocation mechanism in Luchini's treatise studied by (2025), help show which variants were circulating in the region.
  • Test the rival hypotheses against that map: a Byzantine Greek route, a Hebrew route via texts such as the one discussed by (2018), oral transmission through Galeano's circle, and independent derivation from Latin sources. If no carrier is found, the outcome will be a documented negative that shifts the weight between these explanations rather than a proof either way.
  • Progress depends on libraries digitizing and describing uncatalogued Greek and Hebrew astronomical miscellanies in Italian, Polish and Central European collections, including the 's fifteenth-century teaching manuscripts, and on curators willing to examine ownership notes and bindings that images alone cannot show.

archivists and librarians
Examine ownership notes, bindings and library inventories of Greek and Hebrew astronomical miscellanies in Italian, Polish and Central European collections, including the Jagiellonian Library.
philologists and epigraphers
Census every manuscript carrying the Ṭūsī couple or an Ibn al-Shāṭir-type model in any language, recording which device variant each witness carries and its date.

Existing work

  1. 1959
    E. S. Kennedy and Victor Roberts, The Planetary Theory of Ibn al-Shāṭir, Isis 50 (3): 227–235.First showed that Ibn al-Shāṭir's lunar and planetary models are mathematically equivalent to those Copernicus later used.
  2. 1968
    Otto Neugebauer, On the Planetary Theory of Copernicus, Vistas in Astronomy 10: 89–104.Analysed Copernicus's models as technical modifications of Ptolemy and drew attention to their parallels with the Maragha school.
  3. 1973
    N. M. Swerdlow, The Derivation and First Draft of Copernicus's Planetary Theory: A Translation of the Commentariolus with Commentary, Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 117 (6): 423–512.Reconstructed Copernicus's path to heliocentrism from the Commentariolus and argued that his devices were very likely borrowed from Maragha sources.
  4. 1984
    N. M. Swerdlow and O. Neugebauer, Mathematical Astronomy in Copernicus's De Revolutionibus, New York: Springer.Standard technical commentary; argued for transmission and identified the Ṭūsī-couple diagram in the Byzantine text of Vat. gr. 211 as a possible route.
  5. 1995
    Mario Di Bono, Copernicus, Amico, Fracastoro and Ṭūsī's Device: Observations on the Use and Transmission of a Model, Journal for the History of Astronomy 26 (2): 133–154.Argued that versions of the device circulated in a Latin tradition and that independent derivation cannot be excluded.
  6. 2007
    George Saliba, Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance, Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.Made the broadest case for transmission of Maragha-school astronomy to Renaissance Europe, including through Greek and Arabic manuscripts in Italy.
  7. 2007
    F. Jamil Ragep, Copernicus and His Islamic Predecessors: Some Historical Remarks, History of Science 45 (1): 65–81.Argued that both the technical devices and the philosophical motivations of Copernicus's reform have Islamic precedents.
  8. 2014
    Robert Morrison, A Scholarly Intermediary between the Ottoman Empire and Renaissance Europe, Isis 105 (1): 32–57.Identified Moses Galeano, a Jewish scholar active in the Veneto around 1500, as a carrier of Islamic astronomical ideas resembling those of Amico and Fracastoro.
  9. 2014
    Viktor Blåsjö, A Critique of the Arguments for Maragha Influence on Copernicus, Journal for the History of Astronomy 45 (2): 183–195.Argued that the technical parallels do not require transmission and that the documentary case is absent.
  10. 2016
    F. Jamil Ragep, Ibn al-Shāṭir and Copernicus: The Uppsala Notes Revisited, Journal for the History of Astronomy 47 (4): 395–415.Re-read Copernicus's notes in an Uppsala volume and argued that Ibn al-Shāṭir's models had features that made them useful for a heliocentric transformation.
