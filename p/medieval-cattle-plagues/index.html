Did Europe’s three great medieval cattle plagues come from the east?

Cattle died across medieval Europe in three great waves. Finding where each began would help explain how farming communities lost their plough teams and herds so quickly.

Disease · Global · 809–1325

Could use help from philologists and epigraphers and GIS specialists.

Why it matters

An ox could supply a household with years of ploughing. Losses over a wide area left farmers with few places to buy replacements. Timothy Newfield traced outbreaks in 809–10, 937–42 and 1315–25 and considered evidence that disease reached Europe from the east, as it did in some later cattle plagues.

Why it is open

Medieval writers sometimes distinguished a plague of cattle from a winter that killed every kind of livestock. Others simply wrote that animals died. A pilot found that one often-cited Arabic notice in 937–38 gives no location, while several accounts cited for the fourteenth century describe hunger or severe weather.

What would count as a solution

For each wave, establish the earliest independent notices that specify cattle deaths, with a place and a date range. A proposed direction should fit the order of those notices and allow for differences in what local writers recorded.

Potential approach

  • Read the Latin and Irish annals alongside Arabic, Syriac, Byzantine and Chinese accounts. Record the original wording for the animals and the event.
  • Trace copied passages to their earliest witness and check how each chronicler describes cattle deaths in other years.
  • Plot each wave separately with uncertain dates visible. Compare its course with accounts of famine and winter losses in the same places.
