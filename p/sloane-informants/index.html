Who supplied the medical knowledge that Hans Sloane's catalogues attribute to unnamed people?

Sloane's catalogues attribute remedies and drug samples to 'a Negro', 'an Indian druggist' or an unnamed ship's doctor; few of these informants have been traced to named people.

Science & knowledge · Global · 1680–1753digitization

Could use help from archivists and librarians, computational linguists, philologists and epigraphers and statisticians and data scientists.

Why it matters

Sloane's collections, now divided among the British Museum, the and the , are a principal archive for the argument that early modern European materia medica was built on the knowledge of African, Asian and American practitioners whose names went unrecorded ( 2004, 2017; 2017). That argument rests mostly on aggregate patterns and a small number of well-documented cases, so it cannot yet say how much of a catalogue such as came from such people, or whether anonymity was imposed in London, inherited from intermediaries or reflected what suppliers chose to disclose. Each identification also restores a person, place and practice to the history of medicine, and the method bears on how museums now record provenance and attribution for these objects.

Why it is open

Sloane's catalogues usually name the person who passed on a specimen or description, typically a European correspondent, ship's surgeon, apothecary or collector, and refer to the original practitioner, if at all, by category: 'a Negro', 'the Indians', 'an Indian druggist', 'the Dr of the East India Ship'. The documents that could name them are dispersed across 38 volumes of indexed mainly by writer, the acquisition lists of William Courten (whose collection passed to Sloane in 1702), colonial council records and East India Company ship papers, while single catalogue entries and specimens often combine material from several suppliers. No one has systematically listed the anonymous entries, so there is no baseline against which progress can be measured.

What would count as a solution

An individual informant counts as identified when the attribution rests on at least two independent lines of evidence, at least one of them documentary (correspondence, council minutes, account books, ship or acquisition lists) rather than textual resemblance alone, and has been checked against prior scholarship and against the possibility that the entry combines several sources. The problem as a whole is solved to the degree that a reproducible count exists of the anonymous and partially attributed entries in a defined catalogue such as , together with the share of them traced, under that standard, to a named person or at least to a specific place and intermediary.

Potential approach

  • The baseline can start from the 's open transcription of (, Jarvis and Carine, 2023 onward), about 12,750 rows with fields for named collections and other individuals but no tag for anonymous informants. Teams could classify every entry's attribution (named, anonymous category, 'from the same', none) against the manuscript facsimiles, since the transcription can omit words and place-names, and publish the rules and a hand-checked sample so that the count can be reproduced.
  • Matching then proceeds supplier group by supplier group. Courten's lists of things bought, edited by (2017), record dates, prices and sources such as 'the Dr of the East India Ship'; the can be searched through the index; governors' dispatches about remedies bought from enslaved healers are calendared in the ; and the East India Company's list voyages and surgeons. (2012) on how Sloane listed people, (2011, 2013) on slave-ship surgeons as collectors, (2016) on Company servants and their Asian informants, and Pickering (2021) on how the specimens were used give the context for each route.
  • The main risks are description transfer and over-identification. et al. (2025) show that one catalogue number can cover samples and descriptions from several suppliers, and a remedy's description can migrate to a neighbouring entry during labelling or copying. A parallel between a catalogue description and a named printed recipe or colonial record is a lead, not an identification: each candidate should be published with its alternatives, the evidence that would refute it, and a check against Pickering's thesis and curatorial files, and some informants will be resolvable only to a place or community.
  • Because identifications are separable, results can accumulate informant by informant in a shared register linked to NHM object numbers and the , with the baseline count recalculated as entries are reviewed.
  • Progress depends on digitization in collaboration with the and the Natural History Museum: open images of the Sloane correspondence (MSS 4036–4069, 4075–4078), now mainly on microfilm, together with systematic photography of original specimen labels in the Vegetable Substances collection.

Who can help

archivists and librarians
Image and item-level catalogue the Sloane correspondence volumes not yet indexed by the Sloane Letters Project, and photograph original labels on Vegetable Substances specimens with anonymous attributions.
computational linguists
Classify the attribution of every Vegetable Substances entry against the manuscript facsimiles, flagging composite entries and 'from the same' chains, and publish coding rules with a hand-checked sample.
philologists and epigraphers
Identify the languages and likely regions behind vernacular drug names in anonymous entries, from Portuguese, Persian, South Indian and Chinese lists, to narrow where each supplier worked.
statisticians and data scientists
Design a stratified sample of anonymous entries for manual review so the share traced to named people can be estimated with stated uncertainty and tracked over time.
